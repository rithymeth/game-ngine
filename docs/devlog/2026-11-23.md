# 2026-11-23

A global wind, `Wind`, a scene component (renderer/components.h, with the lights): `direction_degrees` (0 toward +X, 90 toward +Z), `strength` (0 calm to 4 a gale, a multiplier on
every material's sway), `gust_speed` and `gustiness` (the share of the sway that is the slow, uneven swell that varies along an object). The first Wind in the world is used; a
world without one has the breeze it had before, 27 degrees at strength 1. It is in the frame constants (`wind`, `wind_gusts`; 1232 to 1264 bytes) with its values clamped and
not-a-number replaced by the defaults.

The vertex shader reads it. The wind blows in the world, but a sway is added to a position in the object's own space, so the world direction goes through the inverse of the model's
linear part (the stored normal matrix is its inverse transpose, so the inverse is that transposed). A side effect, and a better one: it also divides out the object's scale, so the
sway is in world metres however big the model is made (before, a tuft at scale 2 swayed twice as far, because the sway was in local units). The direction is now a unit vector
(it was (1, 0.5), 1.12 long), so the default breeze is about 10 percent gentler than it was.

Checked: CPU tests for the component (defaults, the first one used, clamps and NaN, the scene file round trip, the layout against the HLSL), and a GPU test that the wind is a world direction:
a tuft turned half way round sways the same way on screen as one that is not (42 and 38 px against a background render without grass), a wind toward the camera moves it under 1 px
sideways. A negative control: with the world-to-local conversion removed the turned tuft sways -87 px (the mirror) and the test fails.

Not done: no wind zones (one global wind; an area with its own wind, or a wind that falls off, would be a volume); the shadows still do not sway; no wind on the editor's panels (the component
is editable like any other, but the Procedural World bake does not add one, so baked worlds have the default breeze); particles and cloth do not read it.

## Baked worlds carry a Wind

The Procedural World editor's Output tab now has "Add a Wind to the scene" with a direction and a strength. A bake writes
a Wind entity into the `.ascene`, so the swaying grass and shrubs in a baked world blow the way you set rather than the
default breeze. Turn it off to keep the old scene exactly. The test loads a baked scene and checks one Wind with the
chosen values.

## Wind zones

A `WindZone` entity is a sphere (radius, direction, strength, gust speed, falloff) where the wind is the zone's own,
fading back to the world's Wind over the outer share of the radius. An object takes the zone it is most inside, by where
its origin stands; the frame constants carry up to 7 zones and each draw names its zone and how far inside it is.

The bits came from the cutoff, which was a half in 16 bits and is now 8 bits (a mask edge moves by at most 1/510), freeing
bits 24-31 of the packed options. The test puts an east wind everywhere and a toward-the-camera zone around a tuft: the
tuft's sideways sway goes from 38.49 px to 0.87 px. With the shader ignoring the zone the same test reads 38.49 px and fails.
Not done: shadows do not sway, and a zone is a sphere centred on its entity (no boxes, no rotation).

## Shadows sway with the grass

A swaying plant now casts the shadow of where it is drawn. The CPU turns the wind (the world's, or its zone's) into a sway
vector in the object's own space and the shadow shader moves each vertex exactly as the scene shader does; the two share
the gust formula and the phase.

Swaying casters join the skinned meshes in the cascades only, and the virtual shadow pages left of their spheres fall back
to the cascades: the pages hold what stands still, and a shadow that moves every frame would redraw them every frame.
That is the cost to know: a field of wind-flagged grass is shadowed by the cascades, not the pages. A caster whose wind is
calm (strength 0) stays in the pages.

I found the first version casting no shadow at all in the default setup: the pages had stopped drawing the caster but their
bookkeeping still counted it as standing still. Measuring the shadow's own pixels (the ground's blue-tinted shade) gave
7.06 px of movement over 40 ticks for the swaying tuft and 0.00 for one with no sway. With the shadow shader's sway
removed the same test reads 1.21 px and fails.

## Render textures with a depth buffer (the first step toward water reflections)

Water that reflects the hills and not only the sky needs the scene drawn mirrored about the water's plane into a texture, and
that pass needs a depth buffer or the mirrored terrain draws over itself. The RHI's render textures had none (colour only,
no depth), which is what blocked reflections, so this is the step that unblocks them and nothing else.

`IDevice::CreateRenderTextureWithDepth` makes a colour texture with a D32 depth attachment of its own; `BeginTexturePass`
on it clears both, and pipelines made with `render_texture_target` plus the new `render_texture_depth` depth-test like the
scene's. Vulkan has it; D3D12 returns an invalid handle (the default), so the reflection pass will simply not run there yet.

The test draws two full-screen triangles at different depths into one: the near one wins in either order, a pipeline
with the depth test off lets the last one drawn win, and a second pass starts from a cleared depth. My first version of it
read garbage because a push-constant block with a `float3` after a `float` takes 16 bytes for the `float3` in SPIR-V, and
then because the test's own initializer list dangled; neither was the RHI.

## Water mirrors the scene

Water now reflects the terrain, rocks and buildings beside it, not only the sky. Where water is drawn and the camera is above
it, the scene is drawn a second time into a render texture with a depth buffer (the step before this one), mirrored about the
water's plane, from the real camera. So a water pixel finds its reflection at its own screen position, nudged by how far the
ripples tilt its normal, and the Fresnel term decides how much shows: little looking straight down, more at grazing angles.
Where the mirror drew nothing (the sky) the sky lighting the water already had stays.

How it is done, so the traps are written down: the mirrored world is drawn with the real camera's lighting (only where a
surface is drawn is mirrored, not how it is lit), the projection's near plane is tilted onto the water's plane (an oblique
frustum) so nothing under the water is drawn, and the pass gets its own copy of the frame constants flagging that winding is
reversed, so double-sided surfaces (the terrain) light the right way up. Plants that sway, skinned meshes and water are left
out of the mirror.

Two things went wrong on the way. Each sheet of water must be its own primitive: the baker had merged the sea and a lake
at another height into one, and the mirror's plane is one height, so the baker now writes one flat sheet per level and the
player mirrors the biggest flat one. And I lost an hour to debug colours that the lines after them overwrote.

Limits: one water level per frame (the biggest flat sheet), point and spot lights in the mirror use the wrong light cells, and
D3D12 has no mirror yet (no render texture with depth there). `AETHER_RENDER_REFLECTIONS=0` turns it off.

## Recording a cinematic: `--camera-path` and `--record-dir`

The player already runs scene sequences live (a `SequenceComponent` plays a `.asequence`), but it could not film anything, so filming a world meant one process per frame. (An earlier
note in this file and in a PR description said the sequencer was not wired into the player; that was wrong. What was missing was recording.)
`aether_player --camera-path path.json --record-dir frames/` moves the scene's camera along keyframes (position, yaw, pitch,
roll, field of view; Catmull-Rom, headings turn the short way round), advances game time by 1 / fps a frame so water and wind
follow the film's clock, runs four unrecorded frames at the first pose first so resources are in, and writes every frame as
`frame_00000.bmp`. The file is checked and refused with a reason when it is bad (times that do not rise, a missing
position). The CPU tests cover parsing, passing through the keys, smoothness and the short way round.

Frames are large (a 1920 x 816 BMP is 6 MB), so the recording run converts them to JPEG as they appear and ffmpeg encodes them.
At about 1.5 s a frame on the software Vulkan renderer, 15 s of film is nine minutes.

## A larger world for the opt-in bake

`ProceduralEditor_OptInBakeOfAFullSizeWorld` also bakes `HeroValley`: 576 m across, alpine ridges to 140 m above a sea, lakes
and 6,000 placed objects. It is what the cinematic was shot in.

## The brown band through the sea

The cinematic had a stair-stepped brown band of bare ground running through open sea. Two guesses about the seabed colour and the
shallows' opacity changed nothing, and switching the reflection off changed nothing, so the cause was geometry. The ground
there is about 12 m under the 23.9 m sea, so it was not a ridge either.

The cause: `FillDepressions` spills at the map's edge, so a basin under the sea that spills lower than the sea (here 18.4 m) was
marked a lake with its own surface at 18.4 m. The baker drew that lake's water at 18.4 m, below the sea's 23.9 m, and the ground
between the two levels stood out of the lower water as a band. A basin that is under the sea is part of the sea: its level is
now at least the sea's. `TerrainSeaHasOneSurfaceEvenOverBasinsThatSpillLower` checks it on that world (48,745 cells failed it
before the fix).

## A detail layer for the ground (`AETHER_materials_detail`)

The cinematic's last shot, a close mountain face, was soft. A 4096-px texture instead of 2048 made no difference, so the texture
was not the limit: the ground is smooth-shaded geometry, and one texture stretched over 576 m has nothing to show up close.

A material with `AETHER_materials_detail` (`GltfMaterial::detail`, 0 to 1) gets a procedural micro-surface in the pixel shader, from
world position, so it needs no texture and does not move with the object: three scales of value noise (about 0.4, 1.3 and 4.5 m)
make a height whose slope tilts the normal, and a little brightness variation rides on it. It fades out between 40 and 170 m,
where it would only shimmer. The procedural world's terrain material has it at 0.85. The ending now shows a rough, strata-lined
rock face where it was a smooth blur.

Bits: the per-draw option word was full, so the alpha-mask cutoff went from 8 bits to 5 (a mask edge moves by at most 1/62) and
three bits carry the strength in sevenths. A flat sun-lit plane shows 0.000 mean step between neighbouring pixels without the layer
and 0.731 with it. Cost: about twelve noise lookups a pixel on flagged materials within 170 m, none elsewhere.
Not done: it is one look (rock-and-soil grain) for every flagged material, not per-biome, and the strength is in sevenths.

## Exponential height fog

A `PostProcessVolume` gains `height_fog_density`, `height_fog_falloff` and `height_fog_base`: mist that is that thick at the base
height and thins by a factor of e every 1 / falloff metres above it, so it pools over water and in valleys and leaves the ridges
clear. It adds to the existing distance haze and uses the same colour (the sky's, with the sun's glow toward it).

The post pass already reconstructs each pixel's view-space position from the depth copy, so the fog needs only the world-up
direction in view space and the camera's height above the base. The optical depth along the ray has a closed form,
`density * exp(-k * camera_height) * (1 - exp(-k * climb * distance)) / (k * climb)` (the plain distance when the ray is level),
so there is no ray marching. The push constants were 112 bytes and are now the 128 the API guarantees: the two new settings ride
in the free `w` lanes of the fog and sun colours, and the up vector and camera height take a new `float4`.

The test stands a 70 m red pillar 30 m away in mist: the foot keeps 8% of its colour and the top 28%, relative to the same scene
with no fog. In HeroValley, 0.0035 per metre at the sea's height with a falloff of 0.045 gives a layer of mist on the water with the
mountains clear above it.
Limits: it is a post effect on opaque pixels (the sky and transparent surfaces drawn after the depth copy are not fogged by
height), and it has no light shafts or animated drift.

## Plants take a tint from where they stand

A forest of one tree kind is hundreds of copies of a few meshes, and copies of one colour read as a pattern from the air. Every
instance of a material with wind (grass, shrubs, tree leaves) now multiplies its base colour by `InstanceTint(position)`: a
value change of about +-12% and a slight swing between warm and cool, from a hash of where the instance stands (on a decimetre
grid, so a plant that moves a hair keeps its colour, and the same place is the same tint every frame and every run). It costs
a hash per draw and no extra meshes or memory. The test checks it is steady, bounded to 0.78 to 1.22, averages 1, and differs
from place to place (4,000 places, over 3,500 distinct).
Not done: bark, rocks and buildings are not tinted (only wind materials are), and the tint is not a separate setting.

## Clouds

Every sky so far was a smooth gradient. `SkyLight` gains `cloud_coverage` (0 is a clear sky and the default, so nothing existing changes),
`cloud_scale` and `cloud_speed`: a deck of procedural clouds in the sky shader, in five octaves of value noise. The view ray is
projected onto a flat deck (its x and z over how far it climbs, so the clouds close up toward the horizon and fade out where the
projection runs away), the deck drifts with the world's Wind, and a second tap of the noise a step toward the sun says which side
of a cloud faces it: the near side is lit by the sun's colour, the far side is shaded by the sky. The sun's disc is dimmed by
the cloud in front of it. The frame constants grew to 1408 bytes (a `float4` of cloud settings).
The test: a clear sky has 0.040 mean step between neighbouring pixels in the sky rows and a cloudy one 0.651; ten seconds later a
clear sky is the same picture (0.000) and the clouds have moved (8.8).
Limits: one flat deck (no layers, no volume, no light shafts), the clouds do not appear in the water's reflection (the mirror pass
draws no sky; the water keeps its sky lighting there), and the sky's lighting of the scene does not know about them.

## The water mirrors the clouds

The mirror pass drew terrain and objects but no sky, so where the mirror was empty the water kept only its sky lighting, and the
clouds were missing from the sea. `SkyPass::DrawMirrored` draws the sky into the reflection texture first, with the view's axes
reflected about the horizontal plane (so a water pixel's ray, bounced, is the direction the sky is drawn for) and the same sky
shader, so the clouds, their sun-lit sides and the sun's disc are all in the water. The sky's pipeline for a render texture with depth
is made on first use. Where the mirror texture now holds sky (alpha 1) the water mixes it in by the Fresnel weight like the
terrain.
The test puts a clear and a cloudy sky over one lake: the water in front of the camera differs between them by 17.8 levels
with the mirror and by exactly 0.000 without it (the water then lights from the sky and no more), so the clouds reach the water only
through the mirror. Limits: one cloud deck, one plane, Vulkan only (no mirror on D3D12 yet).

## Water swells, and a woodland of other trees

**Swells.** Water was a flat sheet under a scrolling ripple map. A material with `AETHER_materials_water` now also has swells in the
vertex shader: three sine waves, about 17, 10 and 6.5 m from crest to crest, travelling along the world's wind (and a little to
either side) at the speed deep water gives each wavelength (the square root of g k), lift the surface up to about half a metre and
tilt its normal, which the lighting and the mirror's ripple offset both use. The height follows the wind's strength (2 and over is
full, none is a flat sheet), so a calm world has calm water and a stormy one has a swell. The phase is taken in the model's own
space with up along +Y, and the fine ripples ride on top. The test compares the same lake in a calm and a windy world at a coarse scale
(16 px blocks, so the ripples, which move in both, do not count): 17.6 apart, and with the swell forced off in the shader the same
test reads 0.000 and fails. Limits: no horizontal (Gerstner) displacement, so the crests do not sharpen, and no foam on them.

**A woodland.** The opt-in bake test also makes `Woodland`: gentler ground (85 m) under oak, birch and the odd dead tree with
few pines, where HeroValley is mostly pine. A bake's object budget goes to the scatter rules in order, so the first version, with
the new rules last, got no oaks at all and 4,785 shrubs; the oak and dead-tree rules go first and the shrubs are capped. It bakes
732 oaks, 647 birches, 415 pines and 60 dead trees among grass and shrubs, over a bay with a sandy beach.

## Whitecaps on the swells

The water's swells now foam on their crests. The vertex shader hands the pixel shader how near a crest each point is (the swell's height as a share of its
largest, 0 below 62% of it and 1 at the top), scaled by the wind (none below a wind strength of about 0.7, so a light breeze stays clean); the pixel shader
breaks it up with noise that moves with the game's time, so it is lace and not a stripe, and takes the water toward a sun-lit white and opaque. In a windy
lake the water has 3,855 bright, unsaturated pixels against 170 in a calm one, and with the foam forced off in the shader the windy lake reads 151 and the test
fails. It sits beside the shore's foam, which comes from the water texture.
Limits: the crest is found from height alone (no curvature, so the foam does not know which side of a wave is breaking), and the swell has no horizontal
displacement, so crests do not sharpen first.

## Filming a scene's own sequences: `--record-dir` without a camera path

`--record-dir` used to need a camera path. It now also films a normal run: `aether_player --pak game.apak --record-dir frames/ --record-fps 24 --frames 120` plays the
scene at the film's frame rate, a frame of game time each (after four unrecorded frames at time 0 so resources are in), and writes exactly the frames asked for. A
scene's sequences, its animation, water and wind all follow that clock, so a cutscene made with the sequencer and a `CineCamera` comes out frame by frame with
no camera path file at all. Without `--frames` it refuses with a reason.
The test cooks a scene whose `SequenceComponent` slides a red box across the picture in one second, runs the player on it and reads the frames: at 8 frames a
second the box's centre column goes 48, 58, 68, 79, 90, 101, 112, 121 of 160, further along every frame, and there are exactly 8 frames. It also checks the
refusals (no `--frames`, a bad camera path file). The test runs the real player executable, so it is Linux only for now.

## A distinct oak

The woodland bake used the broadleaf kind at another scale for its oaks, so oak and birch read as one tree. `OakTree` is its own kind: trunk 5 to 7% of the height (birch 2.5 to 4%), main limbs fanned sideways by `crown_spread` 2.2 to 2.8 (the old trees are 1), a deeper green. The first try, thick trunk and denser leaves only, measured the same silhouette (crown/height 1.25 against the birch's 1.31), so the test failed and the spread was added. It also went over the 12,000 triangle budget at foliage 1.1 to 1.5, so the oak's foliage is 0.9 to 1.25. Limit: the oak is still the same limb-and-blob generator, so it is broad, not gnarled.

## Measuring before terrain LOD: the camera culled nothing

The roadmap said terrain LOD. Measured first (`PERFORMANCE.md`, "Terrain LOD, or what the camera never sees?"): terrain is 18% of a baked world's triangles and shrubs are 39%,
and removing shrubs takes 17% off a software-rasterized frame while removing grass takes none. So terrain LOD would have been the wrong feature. The real finding was in the
scene pass: it submitted every draw every frame, in view or not. `player/src/view_cull.h` takes the six clip planes from the view-projection and tests each draw's bounding
sphere (grown by a plant's sway); out of view, or under a quarter of a pixel, it is not drawn into the scene. The shadow casters and the water mirror's draws are untouched
(they need what the camera cannot see). 266 ms to 156 ms a frame in HeroValley on the software rasterizer; frames byte-identical on the film clock.
One trap on the way: two screenshots of the same scene on the wall clock differed by up to 7 of 255 in 15,000 pixels (wind follows real time), so my first "culling changes the
picture" reading was noise. Only the fixed film clock (`--record-dir`) gives a comparison that means anything.

## Far levels for plants, and the number I had to take back

Next on the list was mesh LOD for the instanced plants. `player/src/mesh_lod.h` is a greedy edge-collapse simplifier that only chooses indices (no vertex made or moved), so a far level
costs one index buffer. It went through three versions in the tests: clustering lost the tops of thin pines (a tip's triangles all fall into one cell), "keep the farther vertex"
lost them too, and what worked was a surface veto: a collapse is refused if it would take the last triangle of a vertex on the mesh's bounding box. Then the picture showed what the
height test did not: with the tip kept and the rings around it gone, distant pine tops are needles. At 24 px they cannot be seen, so 24 px is the threshold.
Measuring it found two things. First, my culling number in the last PR (-41%) came from a camera with no plant in view, so it was a best case: with the plants in view culling is
-6% of the frame (-15% of the scene pass). The docs say so now. Second, with per-stage GPU timestamps the shadow pass is 58% of the frame, and far levels (-4% of the frame) barely
move it. The next feature is the shadow pass, not more LOD. `AETHER_PLAYER_GPU_TIMING=1` prints the stages from the player.

## The shadow pass was drawing the whole map four times

After far levels, the shadow pass was the biggest cost, and the obvious guess (resolution) was wrong: it barely moves. Counting what the cascades draw showed 1.73 M triangles, and the three
nearer cascades were almost entirely one draw of 300 k: the baked terrain, one primitive whose bounding sphere is the whole map, so nothing could ever cull it. That is also why the earlier
"terrain LOD" would have been the wrong feature: the terrain's cost was never its detail, it was that it could not be skipped. `SplitIntoTiles` cuts it into 8 x 8, with the normals taken from the
uncut ground so there is no seam, and the existing sphere culling does the rest: the frame goes 11 to 14% down, shadows 14%, scene pass 8%, and the picture is byte-identical.
A second idea, dropping casters under one texel of a cascade, dropped nothing (the smallest caster is a few cm), and its first "win" was run-to-run noise of 30 ms, so I measured twice before
believing the tiles.

## Where the software rasterizer stops being evidence

Sweeping the presets after the terrain tiles: the cascades now draw 730 k triangles instead of 1.73 M and the shadow pass is a floor of about 200 ms for its 4,200 draws at every preset, plus
126 ms of fill at Epic. Both are what a software rasterizer charges and neither is what a GPU does, so the next optimisation (instancing the far cascade's draws) has nothing to be
measured against here and was not built. The roadmap's priority 2 asks for budgets on defined hardware, and this is the point where that hardware is needed: the one measurement to take is
`AETHER_PLAYER_GPU_TIMING=1` on the tiled HeroValley on a real GPU. Recorded in `PERFORMANCE.md`.

## Walking on a baked world

Roadmap rows G02 and G03 are about a character moving over level collision. The engine already had the pieces (a Jolt `CharacterMovement` capsule, a third-person Luau controller, a project template that
wires them on a flat floor), and the baked procedural worlds had none of it: the ground was a model with no collider. `BakeOptions::terrain_collision` now adds a static `MeshCollider` over the ground,
built on the same grid as the drawn ground so the feet meet what is seen. The test drops a capsule on four walkable lines of a baked map and walks it: feet 0.00 to 0.31 m of the sampled ground along
46 m. Two things I got wrong first: the test spots were on 41 to 51 degree hills, where the character correctly slides (a bad test, not bad collision), so spots now need a walkable 10 m line;
and the collider at the drawn grid cost +2.4 s of startup on HeroValley, so it has its own coarser grid (default 192: +0.5 to 0.7 s, mean gap 6 cm). The next step is the character itself (the
entity, its body, the camera, the controller and the input files) in the bake.
One thing I should have noticed sooner: this development tree was configured with `AETHER_BUILD_PHYSICS=OFF`, so every character and physics test was silently not built in my earlier runs and
the ctest gate of 46 checks ran without them. It is on now (CI has it on); the physics-on run found one failure, in a bake test whose entity count the collider changes.

## A baked world you can be in

`BakeOptions::add_player` (on by default where the build has physics, with the collision) puts a person in the baked world: `templates::InstallThirdPersonKit` writes the Third Person template's controller and
bindings into the project (a file already there is kept), the baker adds a Player (a `CharacterMovement` capsule with the script), its body (a 4-colour figure built from tubes, a sphere and a box, 1.8 m, under 700
triangles), a camera, a sun and a sky (a baked scene had none: it was a place to look at, not to be in), on `FindSpawnPoint`'s ground.
The test bakes a world, loads it as the player would (physics, Luau, the bindings) and plays it: the character lands, jumps 1.5 m and comes down, walks 8 m, and the camera is 6.0 m away and 1.4 m above the
ground. It found two real bugs on its first run. The character walked off the edge of the map and fell 36 m and on (the skirt is scenery; nothing collided there): the baker now puts four invisible walls round the map.
And in the steepest preset the spawn was the highest peak: the search had a 15 degree limit and fell back to "highest"; it now relaxes the limit in steps (15, 20, 25, 30, 35 degrees) and falls back to the
gentlest dry sample. A third: the jump check was run after the walk, on a slope the character slides on and cannot jump from; it runs first, where it spawned.
Left: the camera has no obstruction handling (a hill between camera and character); the Input/ folder must be in the project's always-cook list (the panel says so after a bake); the body is a static figure, not animated.

## A camera that stops at the hill, and a figure that walks

The two things the last note listed as left. The Third Person controller (the template's, which the baked world also uses) now sweeps a sphere (0.3 m, `Physics.SphereCast`) from the character's head back toward where the camera
wants to be and stops the camera short of what it hits; it comes in at once and eases back out at 6 per second so it does not pop. `camera_collision` turns it off, `camera_radius` sets the sphere, and it is wrapped in a
`pcall`, so a build without physics keeps the old behaviour instead of failing. Test: with a wall 3 m behind the character the camera sits 2.62 m from its head, 0.3 m (the sphere's radius) in front of the wall's face, and goes back to
6.0 m when the wall is removed; with the option off it goes straight through (the negative control). The test harness now binds the physics scene to scripts, as the player does; before, no template test could have run a physics call.
The figure is now five parts (a body and four limbs, each limb's origin at its joint) and each limb carries `LimbSwing.luau`, which reads how fast the Player is moving from its Transform and swings the limb about X in step: a leg and the arm on its side
half a cycle apart, easing in and out. In the baked world test the limbs are still when it stands, the legs reach 40 degrees in opposition (the arm against the leg on its side) when it walks, and settle within 2 s of stopping.
A still of the packaged world shows the figure standing on the hillside, the pack on its back, the camera behind. It is a stylised figure, not a skinned character: no feet, no hands, no face.

## Killing the editor mid-save, a hundred times

Readiness row A03 said crash recovery was unproven. The pieces were there (the scene writer goes through a temp file, a backup and an atomic rename; `SaveAutosave`, `LoadAutosave`, an in-process test of the recovery) but nothing had ever
died in the middle of a write. `test_scene_crash_recovery.cpp` starts a child process (the test binary again, filtered to a writer test) that loads a scene, adds 1,500 entities of unsaved work and then saves the scene and its autosave in
a loop; the parent waits until it is writing, sleeps a random 0.5 to 30 ms and sends SIGKILL, 100 times, then loads what is left. Over three runs, 9, 11 and 12 kills left a temp file (died mid-write) and in all 300 the saved scene,
its `.bak` and the autosave were whole, loadable files holding the old scene or the new one. A test that cannot fail proves nothing, so a control runs the same loop against a writer that truncates and writes in place: it broke a file on its first kill.
What it does not prove: the Qt editor's restore prompt (a crash report newer than the autosave) lives in `editor/qt/main.cpp`, which this tree does not build, and a real packaged-editor session is untested.
Things I got wrong on the way: I set the test filter with `setenv` in the parent to start the child, and the test framework reads that variable as it goes, so the rest of the suite ran inside the test; the child gets its own environment now. A new scene has five entities of its own, so
the counts are measured, not assumed. And a bigger payload hit the write window less, not more (serialising is the slow part, the write is fast): 5,000 entities gave no mid-write kill in 40, 1,500 gave about one in ten.

## "Not a readable image"

Readiness row A05 asks for "actionable failures for malformed inputs", so I fed the importers a dozen of them and read what came back. The file importer was fair on a missing buffer and bad on the rest, and the loud failures were
the lesser problem: **five malformed models imported as `ok` with no warning** (no `asset` object at all, glTF 1.0, a mesh with no primitives, an accessor reading past its buffer, a file with no meshes), because the loader skipped what it could not read and
said so only in the log, never in `ImportResult::warnings`. The texture importer gave one sentence ("not a readable image") for an empty file, a text file, a truncated PNG and a 64-megapixel one.
Now: the loader keeps a `warnings` list on the scene, every skip and drop names its mesh, primitive and cause (an accessor that reads past its bufferView says how many elements, what stride and how big the view is), `ResolveAccessor` says why where it used to just return false, the JSON parser's own line
and column are in the message (through a SAX handler, because the exception path had crashed once), `asset.version` is required and 2.x only, an index past the vertices is refused, and the importer adds "no meshes" and "not one drawable primitive".
For textures the first bytes say what the file is: a Git LFS pointer (the realistic one: a repo cloned without `git lfs pull` is full of 130-byte "PNGs"), a web page, WebP/TIFF/DDS and friends (converting is the fix), plain text, or a PNG, JPEG or BMP whose own header gives its size, which tells "too big" from "damaged".
`test_import_diagnostics.cpp` has 8 tests; against the old importers 6 fail (the two that pass are "a good file says nothing", which must stay true: all 27 models of the AETHER-01 game and the repo's models and textures import with no warning).
My own bugs on the way: the texture check used `strlen` on a string that starts with a NUL byte (so every unknown file looked like an ICO), and a stream that read a short file reported itself failed (so every file looked unreadable).
Not done: sound has no importer in the registry (`audio/` loads WAVs itself), so A07 is untouched; and texture references from a glTF to a missing image file are not diagnosed here.

## Old files, round trip

Row A04 asks that old files load, "apply migrations, save, reopen, and compare semantic state", and reject what they cannot read without touching the file. The legacy fixtures were only ever loaded, and with the lenient loader, which skips what it does not know: the test would have passed
with components dropped. Reading how the other tools load scenes found the real hole: **the MCP `load_scene` used that lenient loader, and `save_scene` writes the file**, so a scene with a component the server did not know (a plugin's, or one from a newer engine) loaded with a log line, and saving it deleted the component from the
file; an agent would never have been told. The editor already refuses such a scene (`reject_unknown_components`). Now `SceneLoadOptions::unknown_components` collects the names (the loader reads on to name all of them, adds nothing to the world, then refuses), `load_scene` refuses by default naming each one and how to go on,
`allow_unknown_components=true` loads the rest and returns `skipped_components` with a warning that saving over the file deletes them, the server's `--scene` start-up flag does the same, and the editor's message names them instead of "check the Output panel".
Then the round trip: every legacy scene opens in the editor, saves, reopens and saves again. It found one thing at once: `02_Main.ascene` changed on the second save. The editor names the unnamed entities of an old scene ("Entity 2") and treats that form as a placeholder to replace on the next open, but the entity's own placeholder counted as a
name already taken, so on the second open it became "Entity 2 2": every unnamed entity of an old scene was renamed when it was reopened, once. It lets go of its own name first now; a unit test (opens, saves and reopens four times) fails on the old code on all three reopens and passes on the new.
The test compares the original to the resaved file leaf by leaf (a `$v` marker may change, a field the saver now adds is fine, a project is stamped with the current `engine_version`; a key or a value lost or changed fails).
Not done: save game and settings fixtures (nothing from an older release is kept), fixtures older than the v0.27.6 baseline, and fixtures for the older binary formats (scene v1, mesh v1, animation clip v1) that the format table says still load.

## A broken script, 1,000 frames

Row A09 asks to "introduce a known error and show its file, location, and actionable message". I wrote ten broken doors (a syntax error, a nil index, a call of a method that is not there, `error("the door is jammed")`, a failure in `OnCreate`, in `OnStart`, a script that
returns nothing, arithmetic on nil, an endless loop) and read what the script system recorded. Mostly it was already good: `Scripts/Door.luau:4: attempt to index nil with 'boom' (in OnUpdate)`. Three things were not. **Which entity:** with forty doors in a level the line alone does not say which one; the
error now ends `(in OnUpdate, on entity "Front Door" a0b1c2d3)`. **The endless loop** said `script exceeded its instruction budget ... (in OnUpdate)` with no file and no line, because an interrupt has no Lua caller for `luaL_error` to take a location from; it reads the running function itself now (`lua_getinfo` at level 0) and says
`Spinner.luau:4:`, in the form `ScriptError::Parse` (and so the editor and the MCP tools) already understand. **The flood:** a script that fails in `OnUpdate` fails every frame, and every failure was appended, so 1,000 frames made 1,000 identical entries, a list (and a log) that grows for as long as the game runs.
An error that happens again is one entry now, " (repeated N times)", logged at 1, 10, 100, 1,000..., and the list keeps at most 200 distinct errors (the rest are counted in `DroppedErrors()`, and still logged). Two existing tests had encoded the flood (`Errors().size() == 2` after the same error on two frames) and changed to the new contract.
Not done: the Qt script and Blueprint editors (the coverage ledger lists them as not migrated); hot reload in a packaged game; and a script error is shown by the MCP tools and the log, there is no in-game overlay for it.

## The cooker applies the WAV decoder's limits

## A Scene View

## A move gizmo

The Scene View has a move gizmo: three arrows (world X red, Y green, Z blue) on the first selected entity, the one under the mouse
brighter. Dragging one moves the entity along that axis in world space and converts to the entity's local space under a parent, so a
child moves where the mouse says and its stored position stays relative to the parent. The whole drag is one undo step (the
document merges the uncommitted updates), and a Snap checkbox with a step rounds the dragged axis in world space. The logic is a state
machine with no ImGui in it (`TranslateGizmo`), driven in tests with pixel positions computed from the same projection.

Two negative controls, since the tests passed first time: skipping the parent conversion fails the child test, and committing every
frame instead of merging fails the one-undo test. Not done: rotate and scale handles, plane handles, and moving a multi-selection (the
document tracks one pending drag edit at a time, so a multi-entity drag would record only each frame's change).

## Rotate and scale gizmos

`TranslateGizmo` became `TransformGizmo` with three modes (W, E, R or the buttons above the view). **Rotate** draws a ring around each
world axis and turns the entity about that axis by the angle the mouse sweeps round it; under a parent the axis is converted into the
parent's space so the entity still turns about the world axis. **Scale** puts handles along the entity's own axes, so a rotated
entity scales along where it points, and the scale follows the handle's distance from the origin, clamped just above zero so a drag
through the origin cannot collapse or flip it. Each mode has its own snap step (metres, 15 degrees, 0.1) and each drag is one undo step.

Three negative controls on the rotate tests: dropping the parent conversion fails the tilted-parent test, ignoring the snap step
fails the snapping check. A third control showed something useful the other way: removing my accumulate-the-angle-through-full-turns
code changed nothing, because a rotation repeats every full turn, so the code was pointless and I deleted it (and renamed the test that
claimed to prove it).

## Drop a model into the Scene View

which was wrong and is corrected) already made every row a drag source, with the asset's content path as the payload. The Scene View
is now a drop target for it: a `.gltf` or `.glb` dropped on the view becomes an entity named after the file with a `ModelRenderer`,
placed where the mouse ray meets the ground (y = 0; rounded to the move step if Snap is on; at the camera's target if the mouse looks along
or away from the ground), selected, and the whole placement is one undo step (a transaction around the create and the position).
While dragging, a ring marks where it would land; anything that is not a model says so in the toolbar. The payload name moved into

Checked in tests: the ground-point maths (a projected point leads back to itself, no hit when looking along the ground or above the
horizon), which paths place, and the placement (name, path, position, selection, one undo and redo, a texture refused). Two negative
controls: removing the transaction makes the undo take two steps, and dropping the behind-the-camera check lets a point above the horizon
land on the ground. What no test does is perform the drag with a mouse: ImGui's drag and drop between two windows was not simulated.
Not done: dropping on the hierarchy or an inspector field, and prefabs.

## Render Preview: the real renderer, in the editor

A live viewport needs the scene renderer reworked (it draws a cooked package through a running `Game` into a swap chain), but the real
renderer can already make a lit frame: `aether_player --pak ... --scene ... --screenshot`. Run by hand on a Third Person project it took
about 1.8 seconds, the cook (cached) 14 ms. So the editor has a **Render Preview** tool (`ScenePreview`, `ScenePreviewPanel`): **Render**
saves the scene into the project, cooks the project to `Saved/Preview`, runs the player on that scene for 30 frames with a 60 second
watchdog, decodes the BMP it writes and shows it in the panel through ImGui's user-texture support (`ImageTexture`; experimental in
`imgui_internal.h`). It is a still, from the scene's own camera, on demand: not a live viewport and not the Scene View camera.

What building it turned up:
- **A real engine bug.** The player's screenshot is a top-down BMP (negative height in the file), and `stbi_info` reports that height as
  negative, so `DecodeImageFile` and `DecodeImageFromMemory` refused every top-down BMP as "invalid". Any imported top-down BMP texture was
  affected too. Fixed (the height is taken as its absolute value), with a test that builds both orientations by hand and checks the pixel
  order; it fails without the fix.
- **The Scene tool was not in the project.** It saved under `./Content`, wherever the editor was started, so a preview could never have
  seen it. The workspace now points it at the open project's Content folder (switching projects keeps the open scene), which also makes the
  scene list and Save the project's.
- A first version of the picture check was black and I could not tell whether the render or the texture was at fault. The scene the Scene tool
  makes has no models, so the render really is a flat dark colour; pointing the preview at a template project showed the lit scene in the panel,
  which is what proves the texture path. Both are in this commit's checks: the render is tested for many colours, the texture for its size and update.

Verified in the real shell with a software Vulkan driver (the lit scene appears in the panel), and in tests (a real render, the failures
saying why, the texture's lifecycle, the panel saving first, the project hookup). Each of the new checks was broken once to see it fail.
Not tested: a hardware GPU, a window with a mouse, and a render that outlives a project switch beyond waiting for it.

## The gizmos take a multi-selection

Move, rotate and scale now act on everything selected, not just the first entity. The handles stay on the first; Move shifts all by the same
amount, Scale applies the same ratio to each, and Rotate turns each about the same world axis and swings the others round the first's
position as one rigid group. An entity whose ancestor is also selected is left out of the drag (it rides along with the ancestor) so it is not
moved twice.

This needed a different drag. The old one went through `SceneDocument::SetPosition(..., committed=false)`, which merges each update into the
previous command, so two entities alternating each frame broke the merge. Now the live values are written straight into the components while
the mouse is down (no commands, so the scene is not marked changed until the drag ends), and on release the originals are put back and the
result is recorded inside one transaction: one undo step ("Move 3 entities"), and a press-and-release on the spot records nothing.

The first test of "a parent carries its selected child" passed even with the filtering broken, because with the parent listed first the child still
lands right (the code works from explicit world targets). Only a child listed first is moved against its parent's old position and then carried
again, so the test selects the child first; with the filter removed it now fails, as do the filter test, the one-undo-step check (no transaction)
and the rotate check (others not swinging round). Not done: plane handles, and a local-space option for move and rotate.

## Live Viewport: the real renderer, running inside the editor

The Render Preview was a still. The **Live Viewport** (`editor/src/host/live_viewport.h`, panel in `live_viewport_panel.h`) runs the scene: it mounts the cooked
package, loads the scene into the player's `Game`, builds a `SceneRenderer` on the editor's own Vulkan device with a second, offscreen swap chain, and each frame
ticks the game, puts the scene's camera where the orbit camera is (`SceneViewCamera::Rotation`, new), draws, waits and reads the picture back into an ImGui
texture. Controls are the Scene View's, shared (`HandleCameraInput`). It is registered from the shell as a tool (extension panel), because the workspace has no
device. Measured on a software Vulkan driver (lavapipe) with the whole editor drawing as well: about 12 frames a second at 960x540; the real number needs a GPU.

What it is not, stated plainly: it is not the editor's in-memory scene drawn live. The renderer still draws a cooked package through a running `Game`; this
borrows that, so an edit shows after **Reload** (save, cook, load), and the game's physics and scripts run. Making `SceneRenderer` take a `World` is the
rework that would remove both limits, and it is the next step; it is now a smaller one, since the device, the offscreen target, the readback, the texture and the
camera plumbing are done and tested.

Building it found two real bugs, both fixed in the same change:
- **Saving over another scene.** My first run of the hook could not open the template scene (below), and then `Start` saved the Scene tool's untitled default scene over
  the project's `Scenes/Main.ascene`: the Render Preview from earlier today has the same hole (its button saves first and the default path is Main). A plain save now
  refuses to replace a file that is not the open scene; Save As a second time on that name, or an explicit `overwrite`, goes ahead. The Render and Start buttons
  therefore need the project's scene opened first and say so otherwise.
- **The Scene tool could not open template scenes**: modules' components were not registered in the editor process, and an unknown component is refused. Fixed by
  `RegisterEditorComponents()` (a copy of the MCP's list; the MCP cannot link the editor library, so they are two lists). After the fix, the template's 14-entity scene
  opens and saves with nothing lost (the editor only adds a name to each unnamed entity).

Also: a tool an extension adds only exists after the workspace's first frame, so asking for it by name at start (the remembered layout, or `AETHER_EDITOR_TAB`) found
nothing, and the layout was then saved with the default tool, overwriting the remembered one. The shell now retries for a few frames and does not save until it resolves.

Verified in the real shell on a template project (the lit scene, seen from the orbit camera, in the panel) and in tests: the camera rotation against the view matrix, the
components, the in-process viewport on a real device (frames, the picture following the camera, failures saying why, unload and reload), the panel, and the save guard.
Each new check was broken once to see it fail. Not verified: a hardware GPU or a window, and the Windows installer (no Windows machine here; still pending).

## The Live Viewport shows edits without a save

The first Live Viewport ran the project as last cooked, so an edit needed Save, a cook and a reload. The seam that removes all three turned out to be tiny: `Game::LoadScene`
reads the scene through `GamePackage::ReadContent`, so a content override there (`SetContentOverride(path, bytes)`) lets the editor hand the running game its in-memory
scene (`SaveSceneToMemory`, the binary form Play-in-Editor snapshots with, which `LoadScene` already accepts). No renderer rework was needed for this: the cook now only has to
find the *models* the scene names (they are passed to the cook as extra roots), and the scene itself never touches the disk, so the "a save replaced the project's scene"
hole closes for this tool as well (the Render Preview still saves, and still goes through the guard).

To know when to reload there is a revision counter: `CommandStack::Revision` (every recorded command, merged or not, every undo and redo) plus `SceneDocument` bumps for a new or
loaded scene and `Touch()`. The gizmo writes straight into components while the mouse is down (no commands, as of the multi-selection change), so it calls `Touch()` each frame and the
view follows a drag. The panel reloads at most every 100 ms, freezes the game's clock by default (`Run game` on lets it run), and counts the models the scene names that the last cook
did not include, so it can say "press Reload" instead of silently drawing nothing for a model it does not have.

Seen in the real shell on a template project: five model entities deleted in the editor (the ground slab and four cubes) were gone from the live picture a moment later, and the scene
file on disk was byte-for-byte unchanged. A test order bug showed up on the way (the first panel test opened a template scene before anything had registered the modules' components;
it passed only when another test had run first), so the tests now register the components themselves.

Limits, honestly: each change reloads the whole scene, so the world and physics start over (a small scene is milliseconds, a large one is not); material *asset* edits in the Material
editor are not pushed; selection and gizmos are still drawn on the wireframe view, not on the rendered picture. Controls (each broken once, failing the right test): no reload on change,
merged edits not counted in the revision, the override ignored.

## The tools work on the rendered picture

The Scene View's mode buttons, snap, W/E/R, gizmo press and drag, click-to-select and gizmo drawing were all independent of what is behind them, so they moved into a shared
`ViewportTools` (`world/viewport_tools.h`) and both the Scene View and the Live Viewport use it. The Scene View's panel moved to its own header (`scene_view_panel.h`; otherwise
it and the new header include each other) with the same behaviour. In the Live Viewport the overlay is drawn over the rendered image with the same camera and aspect as the picture:
the selected entity is outlined in orange and carries the gizmo, and dragging a handle writes into the scene, which reloads into the game a moment later, so the picture follows.

Checked in the real shell (the orange outline and the gizmo sit at the base of the rendered pillar, in move and in rotate mode) and in tests:
- real mouse events driven a frame at a time through the tools: a click selects what is under the mouse, ctrl adds and removes, empty space clears, selecting records nothing; a press on a handle,
  a drag and a release move the entity and make one undo step, nothing is recorded while the mouse is down, a press and release on a handle without moving keeps the selection, scale with snapping;
- an alignment test against the real renderer: the pixel the overlay camera projects the player's origin to has the rendered blue pillar on it.

Two controls failed to bite at first and were strengthened: the alignment test used the camera target as the player's position, which is the screen centre where flipping Y changes nothing (now
the camera looks beside the player and the search window is about the pillar's height), and the drag test passed with a handle press also counting as a click because the dragged entity ends up
under the cursor anyway (now it also presses and releases a handle without moving). With the Y flip and with the press counted as a click, the respective tests fail.

Limits: picking is by entity origin (as in the Scene View), so an object is selected by clicking near its origin; not by the pixels of its shape, which would need the renderer to write an object-id
buffer.

## Picking by what an object really occupies

Picking was by origin: within 14 pixels of an entity's origin. A large model could not be picked by clicking on it, and its outline was a one-metre cube whatever it was. `ModelBoundsCache` now reads each
model's box from its glTF (every mesh instance's vertices through its node transform, once per path, remembered including "not found", forgotten when a scene is opened or the viewport starts), and
`PickEntity` casts the mouse ray against the entities' boxes (`RayHitsEntityBox`: the ray moved into the entity's local space with the inverse of its world transform, so scale and rotation are right, then
the slab test). The outline draws the same box. The shared tools carry the cache, the Scene tool owns it with the content root, and both views pass it through.

A first version put every box hit ahead of every origin hit, so a camera icon in front of a large model selected the model behind it; my own test caught it. The rule is now nearest along the ray.
In the real shell the selected pillar's outline hugs it ("Player Body" is a child of the Player; the Player itself has no model and shows a unit box, which is correct). Each of three controls fails the right test:
ignoring the bounds, a box always beating a nearer origin, and ignoring the entity's transform in the ray test.

Still a box, not the shape: pixel-exact picking would need the renderer to write an object-id buffer.

## Gizmos in local space

Move and Rotate always used the world axes, so a rotated crate could only be pushed along its own length by guessing at a combination of arrows. The gizmo now takes a
space: World (as before, still the default) or Local, in which the arrows and rings sit on the entity's own axes (Scale already did). The toolbar has a World/Local button
and Q flips it. Snapping needed one decision: in World space a move snaps the position to the grid, but along a tilted axis the grid means nothing, so Local snaps the
distance moved. The test turns an object a quarter turn, checks the arrow is hit where its own axis is and not where the world axis is, drags it, undoes it as one step,
snaps it, and turns it about its own axis; making Local fall back to the world axes fails it.

## Plane squares on the Move gizmo

Pushing something along the floor took two arrows and two drags. The Move gizmo now has three small squares between the arrows (coloured for the axis each is flat
against): grab one and the object follows the mouse across that plane. The drag is where the mouse ray meets the plane through the object, so it is one undoable step like the
arrows, follows the World/Local choice, and with snapping on each of the two directions rounds on its own. A plane seen edge-on cannot be dragged (the ray never meets it), so
the press is refused rather than flinging the object. One decision from the test: the squares sit near the arrows, and from some cameras an arrow is drawn right across a
square, so a press within a few pixels of an arrow's line takes the arrow and only the rest of the square takes the plane. My first version let the square always win and
stole the arrow tip; the Local-space test caught it. Making the drag ignore its second direction fails the new test.

## Moves are patched into the Live Viewport instead of reloading the scene

The Live Viewport loaded the whole scene again on every change, which also restarted physics and scripts on every frame of a drag with **Run game** on. I measured before
building anything (PERFORMANCE.md): for the small template scene a reload is about a millisecond and the frame after it a few ms slower, so for small scenes the saving is modest; at 2,000 entities and 960x540 an edit is
about 116 ms by reload and about 77 ms by patch. The better reason turned out to be the game's state, not the speed.

The patch (`LiveViewport::PatchTransforms`) copies the open document's Transforms into the running world, matched by entity ID, and refuses (changing nothing) when the two do not hold the same
identified entities. Knowing when only transforms changed took a small piece of bookkeeping in the document: `StructureRevision` is `Revision` less what `SetPosition`, `SetRotation`,
`SetScale` and `TouchTransforms` (the gizmo's) added, so the panel patches while it is unchanged and reloads otherwise. An undo is deliberately structural. A first test had me move one small cube and
find almost no pixels changed (194 patched against 242 reloaded, both tiny, so no bug); the test now moves every model. Two controls fail it: a patch that copies nothing, and a `SetPosition` that is not counted.
Still reloads: a light's colour or any other component, because only Transform is patched. Not tried: a real GPU.

## Prefabs can be dropped into the Scene View

The engine could instantiate a prefab (`InstantiatePrefab`) and the editor could expand a level instance, but there was no way to put a prefab into the open scene by hand. `PlacePrefabAsset` does it:
it scans the content folder's asset database, finds the `.aprefab` by path, flattens it, instantiates it with the root at the drop point, and records every created entity inside one transaction as
`CreateEntityCommand`s (the same way paste does), so one undo removes the root and the children together. The instance keeps its `PrefabInstance` link to the asset's GUID, which is what lets a later
change to the prefab propagate. Things I found on the way: `InstantiatePrefab` puts the root last in `created_entities` (so the order is not parents-first, which is fine for recording but not for assumptions),
and the prefab reader silently warns "Skipping unknown component" for a component whose key is not the registered reflected name, which cost one test iteration (the test now takes the key from the registry).
Recording only the root fails the test (the lid is left behind on undo). Not done: the Live Viewport does not accept drops yet, nor does the hierarchy, and the instance takes only the drop position.
Not tried: a real drag with a mouse (the test drives `PlacePrefabAsset`, not the ImGui drop target).

## Light edits are patched into the Live Viewport

The move patch only knew Transforms. A light edit is the same kind of change (a value inside a component the entity already has), so I made the *command stack* tell the two kinds apart
instead of guarding individual setters: `SetFieldCommand` says what it sets (`ICommand::ValueEditTarget`), `CommandStack` counts those apart (`ValueRevision`) and keeps the
(entity, component) pairs since the last look (`TakeValueEdits`, capped at 1,024 and flagged if it overflows), and `SceneDocument::StructureRevision` is now `Revision` less those and the gizmo's direct writes. That
replaced the per-setter guard from the move patch, and it covers the inspector's edits, which go through the stack and not through the document. Undo and redo are never counted, so they stay structural. `LiveViewport::PatchScene`
checks everything before changing anything (the same entities in both worlds, each edited component present on both sides and in a short list of ones the renderer only reads each frame: lights, the sky, wind and wind zones, post-process volumes, decals)
and then copies the edited components through their reflected JSON. A component built into the game at load (a rigid body's Jolt body, the camera) is deliberately not on the list and falls back to a reload.
Two findings from the test: the template scene has a *sky* light and no sun, so the test adds a sun (which reloads, correctly) and edits that, and the first frame after a reload is not a fair reference picture (the renderer's
exposure and history settle over several frames; a fresh reload of the same state differed from it by as much as the patch did), so the test lets the picture settle and compares the patched picture with the dark one. Two controls fail it: copying nothing, and treating the camera as patchable.

## Adding and removing objects without rebuilding the world

Third of the live-update items. Two things made it small. First, the patch from the light edits already knew how to copy a component across; adding or removing a component on an entity that stays is the same
operation with the answer "there on one side only", so `AddComponentCommand` and `RemoveComponentCommand` now name their target like `SetFieldCommand` does (and the names lost "value": `ComponentEditTarget`,
`ComponentRevision`, `TakeComponentEdits`). Second, an entity that came or went needs no list at all: `CreateEntityCommand` and `DestroyEntityCommand` are only counted (`EntitySetEdit`, `EntitySetRevision`), and the
viewport finds the entities by comparing the GUIDs the document has with the set it last saw (`known`, refreshed after a load and after each patch). That also settles a question I would have got wrong: an entity
the *game* spawns by itself (a script making a coin) is in the running world and not in the document, and comparing running against document would have destroyed it. Compared with what the viewport last saw, it is never
touched. An added entity is a snapshot of the document's one restored into the running world (the same call paste uses) plus a GUID index entry, which the game needs to find its parent; a removed one leaves the index too.
The renderer reads models by path each frame, so a new model needs nothing else, and it is drawn if its model is in the cook (the panel already says when it is not).

The safe set is deliberate and short: an entity may be added or removed whole only if every component it carries is one the renderer only reads (lights, sky, wind, post-process, decals, models, names) or the bookkeeping that links it
to the scene and a prefab; a rigid body, a script, a character, audio or a camera is built into the running game at load, so those entities, and edits to those components, still reload. An undo is still structural, so undoing an add
reloads. Three tests encoded the old behaviour (an added sun, an added "Extra" entity) and changed; the model destroyed in the move test carries a physics body, so it still reloaded, which now says why. Measured: 0.5 ms at 2,000 entities for a patch
that adds, removes or does nothing. Three controls fail the new test: not adding the entity, not removing it, and not refreshing the known set (which re-adds everything on the next patch).

## Materials and textures: what the viewport could and could not show

The last of the three live-update items was "show texture and material changes directly in the viewport", and it turned out not to be a viewport question. I looked at how a material reaches the picture before building anything:
the player's renderer (the one the Live Viewport runs) draws each glTF primitive from the material the model carries (base colour factor, metallic, roughness, emissive, a few textures) in a fixed shader, and it has
no material graph; the `MaterialParameters` component that the inspector, Blueprints and Luau already write (per-entity overrides: Set Scalar, Vector, Texture Parameter) was read only by the older
`render_scene.cpp`, never by the player. So an edit to it, in the Live Viewport or in a packaged game, did nothing. That is the gap that is small enough to close properly: the player now applies it,
by fixed names (`player/src/material_overrides.h`): `BaseColor`, `Metallic`, `Roughness`, `Emissive`, `BaseColorTexture` (a content path; loaded before the pass, as models are, because texture uploads must not interrupt a frame
pass). Out-of-range values are clamped and non-finite ones ignored whole. Editing a `.amat` in the Material editor is still not shown in the game: that is a shader-graph feature for the player, not an editor one.

The live part was then small: `MaterialParameters` joined the components the patch may copy, and the cook the viewport makes now includes the images a scene's override names (a path that is not a file is left out, so a typo does
not stop the cook). Checks: CPU tests for the names and clamps; a GPU test that renders a grey box and reads its centre (red base colour makes it red, a green emissive makes green strongest, metallic with low roughness is
brighter and no longer bluish, a blue texture makes it blue, an unknown name changes nothing); an editor test that adds, changes and removes the component on every model and checks the picture follows with no reload, and that a texture
is counted until the cook has it. Four controls fail the right tests: overrides not applied, the texture not used, the component not patchable, textures not in the cook (one control's edit first failed to compile and "passed" on the old binary, so
it was redone). Two of my GPU expectations were wrong and the measurement corrected them: a bright emission tone-maps toward white (so green stays the strongest channel but red and blue rise too), and a mirror-like metal face came out
*brighter* than the diffuse grey, not darker, because it reflects the lit sky.

## Undo and redo are patched too

Every earlier patch left undo as the exception: it bumped the revision once and counted as structural, so after any edit the first Ctrl+Z loaded the scene again, restarting the game state the patches had been keeping. The cause was
bookkeeping, not difficulty. The stack counted a patchable command when it was *recorded*, but an undo or redo of an entry bumps the revision once however many commands the entry holds, so counting its parts would have
made the document's structure revision drift the wrong way (and a later structural change could then look unchanged and be patched when it must reload; I worked that out before writing it). The fix classifies the
*entry*: `ICommand::PatchableChange` answers whether an entry is only component edits and entity creations or destructions and lists the components it touches; a transaction is patchable only if every command in it is; recorded
commands, undo and redo all go through one `NoteChange`, which replaces the two counters from the earlier patches with one (`PatchableRevision`). The viewport needed no change: the entity diff already finds what an undo
created or destroyed, and the component list already told it what to copy. Undo of anything that is not patchable (a model with a physics body, an entity with a camera) still reloads, which the older tests keep checking.
The first version of my test compared pictures after undoing a grouped edit on the sun and passed or failed on noise, because the sun changes this picture by about as many pixels as two settled frames differ; printing the numbers showed that, and
the grouped edit moved to the sky light, whose contrast is thirty times the noise. Two controls fail the new test: undo and redo counted as structural, and a transaction that reports no components.

## The Live Viewport takes drops

The Scene View had the drop and the Live Viewport, the view where a person actually wants to drop a model, did not. Copying the Scene View's twenty lines into a second panel would have meant two copies of the ground-point rule,
the snap rule and the "which paths place" rule, so the drop became a shared `ContentDrop` (`world/content_drop.h`): `DropPoint` (the ground under the mouse, rounded to the step, or the camera's target when the mouse looks along or away
from the ground), `PlaceContentAsset` (a model, a prefab, or a line saying why not) and the ImGui part (`Accept`, `DrawPreview`). Both panels hold one. The two pure pieces are tested; the ImGui target itself is not, because dragging between two windows is not
something the headless harness drives (the same limit the Scene View's drop already had), so the real drag in the Live Viewport is unverified by me. Two controls fail the tests: ignoring the snap step, and placing any path as a model. A placed object then reaches the picture
through the patches from the last few changes (a new entity and its undo are patched, not loaded), so no new live-update code was needed.

## Drops on the hierarchy

Dropping a model or a prefab on a hierarchy row now places it under that entity. The obvious way, place it and then reparent it, is wrong for this editor for a reason that is easy to miss: `ReparentCommand` is structural, so the
transaction holding it is not patchable, and the Live Viewport would reload the scene (and, with it, undo) for every such drop. So the placement helpers take an optional parent and create the entity already parented (`CreateEntity` already
had the argument; for a prefab the `Parent` goes on the root before the creation is recorded, as the level-instance expansion does), keeping it one patchable create. The position is relative to the parent, so Vec3(0) is the parent's origin. A control that
reparents afterwards passes the document test and fails the Live Viewport one, which is what that test is for; the other control (no parent link on a prefab's root) fails the document test. An ImGui drop on a tree row is not driven by a test (the same limit as the other drops),
so the real drag onto the hierarchy is unverified by me; `PlaceContentAsset` with a parent is what it calls.

## The drops, driven by a real drag

Three notes above say the same thing: the ImGui drop target is untested because the headless harness cannot drag between windows. That was an assumption, and it was wrong. ImGui's drag and drop is frame-driven, so a test can press on a button that sets a payload the way a
Content Browser row does, carry the mouse over the target a frame at a time, and release, all through `io.AddMousePosEvent` and `AddMouseButtonEvent` (what the gizmo tests already do). Three tests do that now: a drag onto the view (the marker shows mid-drag at the right ground point, letting go places it
there, snapping rounds it, releasing off the view places nothing, a texture is refused with a reason), onto a hierarchy row (placed under that entity at its origin) and onto the hierarchy heading (placed at the root), plus the heading as the place to drop an entity to make it a root again. Finding the hierarchy's
targets needed ImGui's own record of the target under the mouse (`DragDropTargetRect`, from `imgui_internal.h`): carry the mouse down the list and let go in the middle of the rectangle you want.

What the new tests found: with the heading as plain `TextDisabled`, a drop on it was not delivered in the harness (the target id alternated between two values frame to frame, and ImGui only delivers when the same target took the payload the frame before); the same heading with a real ID is delivered. I do not know that a person
using the editor hit this (a bare `TextUnformatted` heading happened to pass in the harness too and I cannot say why), so I will not claim a user-visible bug, only that the heading is now an item with an ID of its own and the width of the list, which is robust by construction and a bigger target. The tests fail with the old heading, and
a control that ignores the parent on hierarchy rows fails the row test. This also means the earlier "a real drag is unverified" caveats on the Scene View, Live Viewport and hierarchy drops no longer hold for the drag into the Scene View's target and the hierarchy; the Live Viewport uses the same `ContentDrop::Accept`, so the same test covers it, though no test
puts the Live Viewport panel itself under a drag.

## Dropping assets on path fields in the inspector

Every other place that takes an asset got a drop (the views, the hierarchy), but the inspector's path fields still wanted the path typed. All string fields go through two lines of the reflected inspector, so the drop is generic there, but a generic drop on *every*
string would let a model's path overwrite an entity's name, so a field says what it takes: `Meta::asset_extensions` (a ";"-separated list, matched case-insensitively and exactly, so `.png` does not match `.pngx`). Four fields declare it: a sky light's environment image, a decal's texture, a material parameter's texture
and its material. A drop sets the field and marks the edit committed (one undo step, as a finished text entry does), a field that is read only or takes other kinds is left alone (and is not drawn as a drop target, because the payload is only accepted when it fits), and `ModelRenderer.asset_path` is deliberately not one:
it is read only and kept in step with the model reference, which is a GUID dropdown. Tested with real drags onto the inspector (found by carrying the mouse down the value column, as for the hierarchy): a picture lands on the sky's image field, a `.JPG` too, a model and a scene are refused; a material lands on the material field and a picture is refused there; a picture lands on a decal's texture and a material is refused. The material
parameter's texture lives in a list of structs, folded twice in the inspector, which a headless click cannot open reliably, so that field is tested by its declaration, and it is drawn by the same code as the top-level ones. Two controls fail the tests: accepting any extension, and not marking the drop committed.

## Reparenting is patched

Dragging an object under another in the hierarchy was the most common edit still loading the scene again: `ReparentCommand` was structural. A reparent is an add, a change or a removal of one component (`Parent`) on an entity that stays, which is exactly what the component patch does, so the command now names that target and `Parent`
joined the components the patch may copy (it was already allowed on an entity being added whole). The renderer rebuilds its hierarchy snapshot from the world every frame, so a patched `Parent` is picked up with no renderer change. One safeguard: moving an entity under another changes where it is in the world, and a physics body or a script is built from where the entity stood when the scene loaded,
so a reparent of an entity that carries anything the patch does not own (the same test as for adding or removing an entity whole) loads the scene again. The test lifts a big boulder 40 m by putting it under a holder, and checks the picture, the undo, the redo and unparenting (which removes the component) are all patches; moving an entity with a camera under the holder still reloads. Three controls fail it: `Parent` not patchable, the command not
naming a target, and no safeguard. Only the camera case is exercised for the safeguard; a rigid body is covered by the same predicate, not by its own test.

## The other texture slots of a material override

The material override took a base colour texture only. The rest of a material's textures (normal, metallic-roughness, occlusion, emissive) go through the same per-draw packing, so the override now has all five slots by name (`player/src/material_overrides.h`: `TextureSlot`, `TextureOverrides`). Each is loaded before the pass
with the filter its kind needs when the mips are built (sRGB for colour and emissive, linear for metallic-roughness and occlusion, the normal-map filter for normals), keyed by path *and* slot, because one image used as a colour and as a normal map is two textures. Measuring corrected two of my expectations. A metallic-roughness image *multiplies* the metallic and roughness values (glTF), and the model's metallic is 0, so the image alone changed
almost nothing; the test (and the README) now say to set both values to 1 to let the image decide. And a normal map did nothing to the box's front face, because the scene's sun does not reach it at all (the face is lit by the sky alone): the occlusion test gets its large effect from exactly that (a black occlusion image takes the whole face to black), and the normal-map test needed a head-on light
(a `front_light` variant of the scene), where the tilted normal takes about 7% off the face after the tone curve. Each slot has its own control (skipping normal, occlusion, or metallic-roughness and emissive fails the test for that slot). Not done: an `.amat` is still not read by the player, and a slot cannot be *cleared* to nothing (an override replaces the model's image, it does not remove it).

## Picking by the model's triangles

The note on every picking change since the first one said the same thing: by box, and a click beside a thin or concave model still selects it, and the fix would be a renderer object-id buffer. That is one way; a cheaper one needed no renderer: the editor already loads each model to measure its box, so
it can keep the triangles too and test the mouse ray against them. `ModelBoundsCache::Triangles` returns the model's triangles in its own space (each mesh placed by its node's transform), read with the box and kept under a cap of 300,000 triangles (above it a model is picked by its box, since the pick runs on every mouse move for the
hover outline); `RayHitsEntityMesh` is a plain Moller-Trumbore over them, both faces, with the ray moved into the entity's local space and left unnormalised as the box test does, so the two distances are the same units. `PickEntity` keeps the box as the broad phase and refines a box hit with the mesh: a mesh miss is not a candidate, a mesh hit's distance replaces the box's, and
a box that cannot beat the best so far is not refined (the mesh is never nearer than its box).
Three tests: the triangles are kept with the box (24 for two boxes, bad indices skipped), a model of two parts whose box spans the gap is hit on each part and missed in the gap, also through a rotation, scale and move, with a model behind the gap picked and one in front of a part hiding it, and
a model whose wall is far behind its box front is judged at the wall, so a crate between the box front and the wall is nearer. Controls fail the right tests: no refinement, ignoring the entity's transform in the mesh test (these two fail the gap and transform checks) and keeping the box distance (fails only the depth test, which I added because the first version of the tests did not catch it).
What this is not: pixel-exact in the sense of "what the renderer drew". A pose from an animation, an alpha-cut leaf and a vertex shader's wind sway are not in the bind-pose triangles, so those are picked as the solid unposed model. A renderer id buffer would fix those and nothing here prevents adding one. The old origin-proximity pick is unchanged (a click near an object's origin dot selects it).

## Physics bodies are patched in live

Every note on the live-update work said the same thing about physics: an entity with a rigid body or a collider "is built into the running game when it loads", so it reloads. That was an assumption I had not checked. `PhysicsScene::Sync` (called on every physics step) creates a body for a new entity with colliders, rebuilds the ones whose collider or rigid body changed
and destroys the ones whose entity is gone, so the game's physics already follows its world. Putting a collider or a rigid body into the running world, taking it out, or changing one is therefore safe, and the components joined the patchable set (by name, `RigidBody` and the five colliders, so the editor does not link the physics module). What is *not* like that: a script (instances are made when the game starts), a character
controller (`CharacterSystem`) and a camera, which stay on the reload path, as does anything else I did not check. The test counts bodies in the running game (`LiveViewport::PhysicsBodyCount`, the game's own count): a collider on a new entity adds exactly one body, a rigid body on it rebuilds that one body (still one), a collider setting is a patch, undo and redo follow, deleting the entity removes the body, undoing the delete brings it back, and a character component reloads.
Two things it showed. The body count only moves when the game *steps*: with **Run game** off (the default, the world is frozen) the game's Tick runs no fixed steps, so no bodies exist at all and the count stays 0; the control that freezes the game fails exactly there. And the entity handle after an undo of a delete is new, so the test looks the block up again by name. Controls: physics components not patchable (the first
add reloads), and a frozen game (no body ever appears). Not tried: a dynamic body with **Run game** on while editing, where the patch's copy of every Transform would put a falling body back where the document has it (as a reload always did).

## A patch no longer puts every moving body back

The physics note above listed this as untried: with **Run game** on, a patch copied the document's Transform into the running world for *every* entity, so editing anything reset every falling or sliding body to where the document says it is (a reload did the same, which is why nobody noticed). It now remembers the transforms the document had at the last sync (set on load and after each patch) and copies only the ones that differ from that, so a body the physics scene is moving keeps moving when an unrelated object is edited, and an edit to the body's own Transform still reaches it. The test drops a dynamic body from 30 m, lets it fall for a second of game time, edits an unrelated object (the body carries on from where it had fallen to, not 30), then moves the body in the document to 60 m (it jumps there). Two controls fail it at the right check (copy everything, copy nothing that changed).
My first version of the test failed with garbage positions (0, then 1.0) for a reason that was a bug in the new accessor, not the patch: `PositionOf` built the entity map twice, compared iterators from two different temporaries and read from one that was gone. It builds it once now. Not done: while a body is simulated its Transform and the document's disagree, so the Scene View's gizmo for it is drawn where the document has it, not where the game has it.

## Cameras are patched live

Another claim I wrote without checking: that a camera is "set up once when the game starts". The renderer finds the active camera in the world every frame (`FindActiveCamera`), as it finds lights, so a `Camera` component is safe to edit, add or remove in the running world, and it joined the patchable set. What stays on the reload path is the part that really is set up once: a script (instances are made when the game starts) and a character controller (its own system).
The tests used a camera as their "this must still reload" example in five places (an entity that cannot be added whole, a component edit and its undo, an entity and its undo, a reparent, a direct patch), so those changed to a character controller (`MakeWalker`: an entity with `CharacterMovement`, skipped quietly in a build with no physics), with the counts kept the same, and the camera cases became patch checks (a camera field edit, a second camera in and out). The viewport's own camera still overrides the first camera's transform and field of view each frame, so a camera edit changes nothing in the picture; the check is that it does not reload. Controls: camera not patchable (the camera edit and the second camera reload), and the character controller wrongly patchable (five tests that expect a reload fail).

## A model can be picked in the Inspector

## A selected body is outlined where it is

## Scripts are patched live, and restart alone

I had written that a script is "set up once when the game starts", and listed it with the character controller as a reason to reload the Live Viewport. Reading the lifecycle showed otherwise: `ScriptSystem` makes an instance when `Lifecycle::Sync` sees the component and ends it (OnDisable, OnDestroy) when it sees the component go, and `Sync` already runs every tick. So adding a script, or an object with one, needs no reload. What a script does not do is notice an edit: it reads its settings once, when it is made. An edit to a `ScriptComponent` therefore takes the component off, lets `Sync` end that instance, puts it back with the new settings, and the next tick starts it again; only that entity's script restarts, where a reload restarted every script in the scene (and lost their state). Removing an object takes its script off first, with a `Sync`, because the lifecycle ends a script only for a component taken off a living entity (an entity destroyed under it would leave the instance behind). `Lifecycle::Sync` is now public for this, and `LiveViewport` counts `ScriptInstanceCount` and `ScriptRestarts`.

Test (the template's script, on a new entity): adding makes one more instance with no reload, an edit restarts it (one restart counted, still one instance, no reload), destroying the object ends it, undo brings it back. Controls: scripts not patchable fails the add, the edit and the count; skipping the end-before-destroy step leaves the instance behind and fails the count after destroy. Not seen: that the restarted script has the new value (the test counts instances and restarts, it does not read a script's state); the character controller and audio sources still reload; a change to the `.luau` source is not a scene edit and is still the script reload's job.

## Material graphs: step 1, the game reads them and says so

A `.amat` named on a `MaterialParameters.material` was ignored by the game without a word. Reading what stands between a graph and a draw: the cook did not carry the file (nothing claims `.amat` as an asset, so a path on the always-cook list that was not an asset only produced a warning), the player never read it, and the player's one lit shader takes packed per-draw factors, not a generated include, so drawing a graph needs a shader per graph (the codegen, `GenerateHlsl`, exists and is tested; the player uses none of it). That is the whole job, and this is its first step, which draws nothing yet.

The cook now carries a plain file named on the always-cook list (a material graph, a data file) as it is, as it already did the files of a folder, instead of warning that it is not an asset. `player::CompileGraphMaterial` reads the graph from the package, checks it and generates its shader, and reports whether it compiles, its live nodes, textures and parameter bytes, and the shader's permutation key. The Live Viewport lists every graph the scene's materials name with that report under the toolbar ("Materials/Tint.amat: Compiles: 2 nodes, 0 textures; not drawn yet." or why not), so a graph is no longer shown as the model's own surface under its name. A `.amati` is reported as not supported yet.

Test: a valid graph is found and compiles (2 live nodes, 16 bytes of parameters, a key), a graph with no output reports its error, a file that is not a graph says so, a path not in the package says so, an instance says so, and no graph named says nothing. Control: the old cook behaviour fails all of those that need the file. Not done, and next: drawing with the graph (a pipeline per permutation, the graph's constant buffer and textures, bound per draw), then textures, then showing a graph edit live.

## Material graphs: step 2, the game draws with a graph

The first picture from a `.amat`. A `MaterialParameters.material` naming a graph of constants and parameters that is opaque and default-lit is now drawn with the graph: `CompileGraphMaterial` bakes the graph's parameter defaults into constants in its generated shader (no constant buffer to bind, so an edit is a new shader, not a changed value), the scene shader gets a second form that calls the graph's `EvaluateMaterial` after the model's surface is computed and takes base colour, metallic, roughness, occlusion and emissive from it, and the renderer makes that pipeline (and its back-face-culled twin) the first time a draw needs it, keeps it per graph, and draws each graph's models together. A graph that does not build, or that is not drawable, draws with the model's own surface and says so under the Live Viewport's toolbar ("... drawn." or "... not drawn yet (a graph that samples textures is not)."). A material graph's world position, camera vector and time come from the frame constants, so the graph pipelines need a device that has them (Vulkan does).

Test: with the graph on every model, the picture goes from almost no red to most of the frame red (the floor is a model), and a graph that samples a texture leaves the picture as it was. Control: never choosing the graph's pipeline fails the red checks. Screenshots of the red graph and of a metallic gold one (the sun's highlight on the floor) were checked by eye.

Not done: textures in a graph (a texture parameter needs a bound slot per draw), a graph's normal output, translucent and masked graphs, other shading models, a vertex offset, `.amati` instances and per-entity parameter values over a graph's defaults, and showing a graph edited in the Material editor without pressing Reload. Shadows and the reflection pass still draw the model's own surface.

## Material graphs: step 3, textures in a graph

A graph that samples a texture parameter is drawn. Three pieces. The cook: a `.amat` on the cook list now brings the textures its parameters name (by asset GUID, inside the file, where nothing else would have found them). The shader: the graph's `Texture.Sample` calls become `GraphSample(slot, uv)`, a function that reads the scene's bindless table at the slot the draw's push constants carry (the same four 8-bit fields a model's own textures use, so a graph has at most four; a fifth is reported as not drawn). The renderer: the first draw that needs a graph loads its textures through the package's usual path (a missing or unset one is the white texture) and keeps the packed slots with the graph's pipelines, and each draw of that graph puts them in its constants in place of the model's own.

Two things I got wrong on the way, kept because they cost time. A texture wrapped in a struct with `Sample` methods, called on a `static const` instance, makes glslang's HLSL front end emit invalid SPIR-V (an access chain on a non-pointer; `spirv-val` says so, lavapipe crashes in pipeline creation); plain functions and a text rewrite of the sample calls avoid it. And an unbound slot in a graph shader is a crash, not a black pixel, because the graph samples without the model path's "index < 128" guard; the helpers now clamp the slot.

Test: a graph sampling a blue texture asset (found by the GUID the asset database gave it) turns the picture blue from the texture's own pixels; a graph with an unlit shading model still shows the model's own surface. Controls: the cook not following the texture GUIDs fails the blue check (the texture is missing, so white); not putting the graph's slots in the draw's constants crashes the test (the model's own slots are not the graph's textures). Checked by eye with a checker image multiplied by a tint parameter. Colour: a graph's samples are used as the graph says, raw, with no sRGB decode (a graph has no colour-space flag yet), so a colour photo used as a base colour reads brighter than the model path's decode would make it; a power node in the graph corrects it.

Not done: a graph's normal output, triplanar sampling, more than four textures, translucent and masked graphs, other shading models, `.amati` instances, and per-entity values over a graph's defaults. A graph's textures are carried by the editor's preview cook and by an always-cook entry; a packaged game whose only reference to the graph is a scene's material path does not yet carry it.

## Material graphs: step 4, a saved graph shows without Reload

A graph is read from the cooked package when the game first draws with it, so a graph edited in the Material editor needed Reload. Now the Live Viewport looks at the graph files its scene names (in the project's Content folder, a hash of each file's bytes, every time it advances) and hands one that differs from what it last saw to the running game, as that content file (`GamePackage::SetContentOverride`, the same way the open scene is served from memory). The game's renderer forgets every graph's shader (`SceneRenderer::ReloadGraphMaterials`) and the next draw builds them again. A graph that is not in the package at all, because the scene named it after the cook, is handed over the same way, so naming a new graph needs no Reload either (its textures still need one, as they are cooked). The device is idle at that point: `LiveViewport::Frame` waits for its fence before it returns, and the pipelines are replaced between frames.

Test: the picture goes from red to green when the file is saved, with no Reload and no scene reload; saved without an output it falls back to the model's own surface and says why; saved right again it is back; a graph that only exists after the cook shows blue. Control: leaving the old shaders in place after the file is handed over keeps the picture red and fails the green check. Checked by eye: a red rough graph saved as a teal, metallic, glossy one.

Cost: every graph the scene names is hashed each time the viewport advances (a few kilobytes each); a scene naming hundreds of graphs would want this spread over frames. Every graph is rebuilt when any one changes, which is a few pipelines.
