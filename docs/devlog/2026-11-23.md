# 2026-11-23

A global wind, `Wind`, a scene component (renderer/components.h, with the lights): `direction_degrees` (0 toward +X, 90 toward +Z), `strength` (0 calm to 4 a gale, a multiplier on
every material's sway), `gust_speed` and `gustiness` (the share of the sway that is the slow, uneven swell that varies along an object). The first Wind in the world is used; a
world without one has the breeze it had before, 27 degrees at strength 1. It is in the frame constants (`wind`, `wind_gusts`; 1232 to 1264 bytes) with its values clamped and
not-a-number replaced by the defaults.

The vertex shader reads it. The wind blows in the world, but a sway is added to a position in the object's own space, so the world direction goes through the inverse of the model's
linear part (the stored normal matrix is its inverse transpose, so the inverse is that transposed). A side effect, and a better one: it also divides out the object's scale, so the
sway is in world metres however big the model is made (before, a tuft at scale 2 swayed twice as far, because the sway was in local units). The direction is now a unit vector
(it was (1, 0.5), 1.12 long), so the default breeze is about 10 percent gentler than it was.

Checked: CPU tests for the component (defaults, the first one used, clamps and NaN, the scene file round trip, the layout against the HLSL), and a GPU test that the wind is a world direction:
a tuft turned half way round sways the same way on screen as one that is not (42 and 38 px against a background render without grass), a wind toward the camera moves it under 1 px
sideways. A negative control: with the world-to-local conversion removed the turned tuft sways -87 px (the mirror) and the test fails.

Not done: no wind zones (one global wind; an area with its own wind, or a wind that falls off, would be a volume); the shadows still do not sway; no wind on the editor's panels (the component
is editable like any other, but the Procedural World bake does not add one, so baked worlds have the default breeze); particles and cloth do not read it.

## Baked worlds carry a Wind

The Procedural World editor's Output tab now has "Add a Wind to the scene" with a direction and a strength. A bake writes
a Wind entity into the `.ascene`, so the swaying grass and shrubs in a baked world blow the way you set rather than the
default breeze. Turn it off to keep the old scene exactly. The test loads a baked scene and checks one Wind with the
chosen values.

## Wind zones

A `WindZone` entity is a sphere (radius, direction, strength, gust speed, falloff) where the wind is the zone's own,
fading back to the world's Wind over the outer share of the radius. An object takes the zone it is most inside, by where
its origin stands; the frame constants carry up to 7 zones and each draw names its zone and how far inside it is.

The bits came from the cutoff, which was a half in 16 bits and is now 8 bits (a mask edge moves by at most 1/510), freeing
bits 24-31 of the packed options. The test puts an east wind everywhere and a toward-the-camera zone around a tuft: the
tuft's sideways sway goes from 38.49 px to 0.87 px. With the shader ignoring the zone the same test reads 38.49 px and fails.
Not done: shadows do not sway, and a zone is a sphere centred on its entity (no boxes, no rotation).

## Shadows sway with the grass

A swaying plant now casts the shadow of where it is drawn. The CPU turns the wind (the world's, or its zone's) into a sway
vector in the object's own space and the shadow shader moves each vertex exactly as the scene shader does; the two share
the gust formula and the phase.

Swaying casters join the skinned meshes in the cascades only, and the virtual shadow pages left of their spheres fall back
to the cascades: the pages hold what stands still, and a shadow that moves every frame would redraw them every frame.
That is the cost to know: a field of wind-flagged grass is shadowed by the cascades, not the pages. A caster whose wind is
calm (strength 0) stays in the pages.

I found the first version casting no shadow at all in the default setup: the pages had stopped drawing the caster but their
bookkeeping still counted it as standing still. Measuring the shadow's own pixels (the ground's blue-tinted shade) gave
7.06 px of movement over 40 ticks for the swaying tuft and 0.00 for one with no sway. With the shadow shader's sway
removed the same test reads 1.21 px and fails.

## Render textures with a depth buffer (the first step toward water reflections)

Water that reflects the hills and not only the sky needs the scene drawn mirrored about the water's plane into a texture, and
that pass needs a depth buffer or the mirrored terrain draws over itself. The RHI's render textures had none (colour only,
no depth), which is what blocked reflections, so this is the step that unblocks them and nothing else.

`IDevice::CreateRenderTextureWithDepth` makes a colour texture with a D32 depth attachment of its own; `BeginTexturePass`
on it clears both, and pipelines made with `render_texture_target` plus the new `render_texture_depth` depth-test like the
scene's. Vulkan has it; D3D12 returns an invalid handle (the default), so the reflection pass will simply not run there yet.

The test draws two full-screen triangles at different depths into one: the near one wins in either order, a pipeline
with the depth test off lets the last one drawn win, and a second pass starts from a cleared depth. My first version of it
read garbage because a push-constant block with a `float3` after a `float` takes 16 bytes for the `float3` in SPIR-V, and
then because the test's own initializer list dangled; neither was the RHI.

## Water mirrors the scene

Water now reflects the terrain, rocks and buildings beside it, not only the sky. Where water is drawn and the camera is above
it, the scene is drawn a second time into a render texture with a depth buffer (the step before this one), mirrored about the
water's plane, from the real camera. So a water pixel finds its reflection at its own screen position, nudged by how far the
ripples tilt its normal, and the Fresnel term decides how much shows: little looking straight down, more at grazing angles.
Where the mirror drew nothing (the sky) the sky lighting the water already had stays.

How it is done, so the traps are written down: the mirrored world is drawn with the real camera's lighting (only where a
surface is drawn is mirrored, not how it is lit), the projection's near plane is tilted onto the water's plane (an oblique
frustum) so nothing under the water is drawn, and the pass gets its own copy of the frame constants flagging that winding is
reversed, so double-sided surfaces (the terrain) light the right way up. Plants that sway, skinned meshes and water are left
out of the mirror.

Two things went wrong on the way. Each sheet of water must be its own primitive: the baker had merged the sea and a lake
at another height into one, and the mirror's plane is one height, so the baker now writes one flat sheet per level and the
player mirrors the biggest flat one. And I lost an hour to debug colours that the lines after them overwrote.

Limits: one water level per frame (the biggest flat sheet), point and spot lights in the mirror use the wrong light cells, and
D3D12 has no mirror yet (no render texture with depth there). `AETHER_RENDER_REFLECTIONS=0` turns it off.

## Recording a cinematic: `--camera-path` and `--record-dir`

The player already runs scene sequences live (a `SequenceComponent` plays a `.asequence`), but it could not film anything, so filming a world meant one process per frame. (An earlier
note in this file and in a PR description said the sequencer was not wired into the player; that was wrong. What was missing was recording.)
`aether_player --camera-path path.json --record-dir frames/` moves the scene's camera along keyframes (position, yaw, pitch,
roll, field of view; Catmull-Rom, headings turn the short way round), advances game time by 1 / fps a frame so water and wind
follow the film's clock, runs four unrecorded frames at the first pose first so resources are in, and writes every frame as
`frame_00000.bmp`. The file is checked and refused with a reason when it is bad (times that do not rise, a missing
position). The CPU tests cover parsing, passing through the keys, smoothness and the short way round.

Frames are large (a 1920 x 816 BMP is 6 MB), so the recording run converts them to JPEG as they appear and ffmpeg encodes them.
At about 1.5 s a frame on the software Vulkan renderer, 15 s of film is nine minutes.

## A larger world for the opt-in bake

`ProceduralEditor_OptInBakeOfAFullSizeWorld` also bakes `HeroValley`: 576 m across, alpine ridges to 140 m above a sea, lakes
and 6,000 placed objects. It is what the cinematic was shot in.

## The brown band through the sea

The cinematic had a stair-stepped brown band of bare ground running through open sea. Two guesses about the seabed colour and the
shallows' opacity changed nothing, and switching the reflection off changed nothing, so the cause was geometry. The ground
there is about 12 m under the 23.9 m sea, so it was not a ridge either.

The cause: `FillDepressions` spills at the map's edge, so a basin under the sea that spills lower than the sea (here 18.4 m) was
marked a lake with its own surface at 18.4 m. The baker drew that lake's water at 18.4 m, below the sea's 23.9 m, and the ground
between the two levels stood out of the lower water as a band. A basin that is under the sea is part of the sea: its level is
now at least the sea's. `TerrainSeaHasOneSurfaceEvenOverBasinsThatSpillLower` checks it on that world (48,745 cells failed it
before the fix).

## A detail layer for the ground (`AETHER_materials_detail`)

The cinematic's last shot, a close mountain face, was soft. A 4096-px texture instead of 2048 made no difference, so the texture
was not the limit: the ground is smooth-shaded geometry, and one texture stretched over 576 m has nothing to show up close.

A material with `AETHER_materials_detail` (`GltfMaterial::detail`, 0 to 1) gets a procedural micro-surface in the pixel shader, from
world position, so it needs no texture and does not move with the object: three scales of value noise (about 0.4, 1.3 and 4.5 m)
make a height whose slope tilts the normal, and a little brightness variation rides on it. It fades out between 40 and 170 m,
where it would only shimmer. The procedural world's terrain material has it at 0.85. The ending now shows a rough, strata-lined
rock face where it was a smooth blur.

Bits: the per-draw option word was full, so the alpha-mask cutoff went from 8 bits to 5 (a mask edge moves by at most 1/62) and
three bits carry the strength in sevenths. A flat sun-lit plane shows 0.000 mean step between neighbouring pixels without the layer
and 0.731 with it. Cost: about twelve noise lookups a pixel on flagged materials within 170 m, none elsewhere.
Not done: it is one look (rock-and-soil grain) for every flagged material, not per-biome, and the strength is in sevenths.

## Exponential height fog

A `PostProcessVolume` gains `height_fog_density`, `height_fog_falloff` and `height_fog_base`: mist that is that thick at the base
height and thins by a factor of e every 1 / falloff metres above it, so it pools over water and in valleys and leaves the ridges
clear. It adds to the existing distance haze and uses the same colour (the sky's, with the sun's glow toward it).

The post pass already reconstructs each pixel's view-space position from the depth copy, so the fog needs only the world-up
direction in view space and the camera's height above the base. The optical depth along the ray has a closed form,
`density * exp(-k * camera_height) * (1 - exp(-k * climb * distance)) / (k * climb)` (the plain distance when the ray is level),
so there is no ray marching. The push constants were 112 bytes and are now the 128 the API guarantees: the two new settings ride
in the free `w` lanes of the fog and sun colours, and the up vector and camera height take a new `float4`.

The test stands a 70 m red pillar 30 m away in mist: the foot keeps 8% of its colour and the top 28%, relative to the same scene
with no fog. In HeroValley, 0.0035 per metre at the sea's height with a falloff of 0.045 gives a layer of mist on the water with the
mountains clear above it.
Limits: it is a post effect on opaque pixels (the sky and transparent surfaces drawn after the depth copy are not fogged by
height), and it has no light shafts or animated drift.

## Plants take a tint from where they stand

A forest of one tree kind is hundreds of copies of a few meshes, and copies of one colour read as a pattern from the air. Every
instance of a material with wind (grass, shrubs, tree leaves) now multiplies its base colour by `InstanceTint(position)`: a
value change of about +-12% and a slight swing between warm and cool, from a hash of where the instance stands (on a decimetre
grid, so a plant that moves a hair keeps its colour, and the same place is the same tint every frame and every run). It costs
a hash per draw and no extra meshes or memory. The test checks it is steady, bounded to 0.78 to 1.22, averages 1, and differs
from place to place (4,000 places, over 3,500 distinct).
Not done: bark, rocks and buildings are not tinted (only wind materials are), and the tint is not a separate setting.

## Clouds

Every sky so far was a smooth gradient. `SkyLight` gains `cloud_coverage` (0 is a clear sky and the default, so nothing existing changes),
`cloud_scale` and `cloud_speed`: a deck of procedural clouds in the sky shader, in five octaves of value noise. The view ray is
projected onto a flat deck (its x and z over how far it climbs, so the clouds close up toward the horizon and fade out where the
projection runs away), the deck drifts with the world's Wind, and a second tap of the noise a step toward the sun says which side
of a cloud faces it: the near side is lit by the sun's colour, the far side is shaded by the sky. The sun's disc is dimmed by
the cloud in front of it. The frame constants grew to 1408 bytes (a `float4` of cloud settings).
The test: a clear sky has 0.040 mean step between neighbouring pixels in the sky rows and a cloudy one 0.651; ten seconds later a
clear sky is the same picture (0.000) and the clouds have moved (8.8).
Limits: one flat deck (no layers, no volume, no light shafts), the clouds do not appear in the water's reflection (the mirror pass
draws no sky; the water keeps its sky lighting there), and the sky's lighting of the scene does not know about them.

## The water mirrors the clouds

The mirror pass drew terrain and objects but no sky, so where the mirror was empty the water kept only its sky lighting, and the
clouds were missing from the sea. `SkyPass::DrawMirrored` draws the sky into the reflection texture first, with the view's axes
reflected about the horizontal plane (so a water pixel's ray, bounced, is the direction the sky is drawn for) and the same sky
shader, so the clouds, their sun-lit sides and the sun's disc are all in the water. The sky's pipeline for a render texture with depth
is made on first use. Where the mirror texture now holds sky (alpha 1) the water mixes it in by the Fresnel weight like the
terrain.
The test puts a clear and a cloudy sky over one lake: the water in front of the camera differs between them by 17.8 levels
with the mirror and by exactly 0.000 without it (the water then lights from the sky and no more), so the clouds reach the water only
through the mirror. Limits: one cloud deck, one plane, Vulkan only (no mirror on D3D12 yet).

## Water swells, and a woodland of other trees

**Swells.** Water was a flat sheet under a scrolling ripple map. A material with `AETHER_materials_water` now also has swells in the
vertex shader: three sine waves, about 17, 10 and 6.5 m from crest to crest, travelling along the world's wind (and a little to
either side) at the speed deep water gives each wavelength (the square root of g k), lift the surface up to about half a metre and
tilt its normal, which the lighting and the mirror's ripple offset both use. The height follows the wind's strength (2 and over is
full, none is a flat sheet), so a calm world has calm water and a stormy one has a swell. The phase is taken in the model's own
space with up along +Y, and the fine ripples ride on top. The test compares the same lake in a calm and a windy world at a coarse scale
(16 px blocks, so the ripples, which move in both, do not count): 17.6 apart, and with the swell forced off in the shader the same
test reads 0.000 and fails. Limits: no horizontal (Gerstner) displacement, so the crests do not sharpen, and no foam on them.

**A woodland.** The opt-in bake test also makes `Woodland`: gentler ground (85 m) under oak, birch and the odd dead tree with
few pines, where HeroValley is mostly pine. A bake's object budget goes to the scatter rules in order, so the first version, with
the new rules last, got no oaks at all and 4,785 shrubs; the oak and dead-tree rules go first and the shrubs are capped. It bakes
732 oaks, 647 birches, 415 pines and 60 dead trees among grass and shrubs, over a bay with a sandy beach.

## Whitecaps on the swells

The water's swells now foam on their crests. The vertex shader hands the pixel shader how near a crest each point is (the swell's height as a share of its
largest, 0 below 62% of it and 1 at the top), scaled by the wind (none below a wind strength of about 0.7, so a light breeze stays clean); the pixel shader
breaks it up with noise that moves with the game's time, so it is lace and not a stripe, and takes the water toward a sun-lit white and opaque. In a windy
lake the water has 3,855 bright, unsaturated pixels against 170 in a calm one, and with the foam forced off in the shader the windy lake reads 151 and the test
fails. It sits beside the shore's foam, which comes from the water texture.
Limits: the crest is found from height alone (no curvature, so the foam does not know which side of a wave is breaking), and the swell has no horizontal
displacement, so crests do not sharpen first.

## Filming a scene's own sequences: `--record-dir` without a camera path

`--record-dir` used to need a camera path. It now also films a normal run: `aether_player --pak game.apak --record-dir frames/ --record-fps 24 --frames 120` plays the
scene at the film's frame rate, a frame of game time each (after four unrecorded frames at time 0 so resources are in), and writes exactly the frames asked for. A
scene's sequences, its animation, water and wind all follow that clock, so a cutscene made with the sequencer and a `CineCamera` comes out frame by frame with
no camera path file at all. Without `--frames` it refuses with a reason.
The test cooks a scene whose `SequenceComponent` slides a red box across the picture in one second, runs the player on it and reads the frames: at 8 frames a
second the box's centre column goes 48, 58, 68, 79, 90, 101, 112, 121 of 160, further along every frame, and there are exactly 8 frames. It also checks the
refusals (no `--frames`, a bad camera path file). The test runs the real player executable, so it is Linux only for now.

## A distinct oak

The woodland bake used the broadleaf kind at another scale for its oaks, so oak and birch read as one tree. `OakTree` is its own kind: trunk 5 to 7% of the height (birch 2.5 to 4%), main limbs fanned sideways by `crown_spread` 2.2 to 2.8 (the old trees are 1), a deeper green. The first try, thick trunk and denser leaves only, measured the same silhouette (crown/height 1.25 against the birch's 1.31), so the test failed and the spread was added. It also went over the 12,000 triangle budget at foliage 1.1 to 1.5, so the oak's foliage is 0.9 to 1.25. Limit: the oak is still the same limb-and-blob generator, so it is broad, not gnarled.

## Measuring before terrain LOD: the camera culled nothing

The roadmap said terrain LOD. Measured first (`PERFORMANCE.md`, "Terrain LOD, or what the camera never sees?"): terrain is 18% of a baked world's triangles and shrubs are 39%,
and removing shrubs takes 17% off a software-rasterized frame while removing grass takes none. So terrain LOD would have been the wrong feature. The real finding was in the
scene pass: it submitted every draw every frame, in view or not. `player/src/view_cull.h` takes the six clip planes from the view-projection and tests each draw's bounding
sphere (grown by a plant's sway); out of view, or under a quarter of a pixel, it is not drawn into the scene. The shadow casters and the water mirror's draws are untouched
(they need what the camera cannot see). 266 ms to 156 ms a frame in HeroValley on the software rasterizer; frames byte-identical on the film clock.
One trap on the way: two screenshots of the same scene on the wall clock differed by up to 7 of 255 in 15,000 pixels (wind follows real time), so my first "culling changes the
picture" reading was noise. Only the fixed film clock (`--record-dir`) gives a comparison that means anything.

## Far levels for plants, and the number I had to take back

Next on the list was mesh LOD for the instanced plants. `player/src/mesh_lod.h` is a greedy edge-collapse simplifier that only chooses indices (no vertex made or moved), so a far level
costs one index buffer. It went through three versions in the tests: clustering lost the tops of thin pines (a tip's triangles all fall into one cell), "keep the farther vertex"
lost them too, and what worked was a surface veto: a collapse is refused if it would take the last triangle of a vertex on the mesh's bounding box. Then the picture showed what the
height test did not: with the tip kept and the rings around it gone, distant pine tops are needles. At 24 px they cannot be seen, so 24 px is the threshold.
Measuring it found two things. First, my culling number in the last PR (-41%) came from a camera with no plant in view, so it was a best case: with the plants in view culling is
-6% of the frame (-15% of the scene pass). The docs say so now. Second, with per-stage GPU timestamps the shadow pass is 58% of the frame, and far levels (-4% of the frame) barely
move it. The next feature is the shadow pass, not more LOD. `AETHER_PLAYER_GPU_TIMING=1` prints the stages from the player.

## The shadow pass was drawing the whole map four times

After far levels, the shadow pass was the biggest cost, and the obvious guess (resolution) was wrong: it barely moves. Counting what the cascades draw showed 1.73 M triangles, and the three
nearer cascades were almost entirely one draw of 300 k: the baked terrain, one primitive whose bounding sphere is the whole map, so nothing could ever cull it. That is also why the earlier
"terrain LOD" would have been the wrong feature: the terrain's cost was never its detail, it was that it could not be skipped. `SplitIntoTiles` cuts it into 8 x 8, with the normals taken from the
uncut ground so there is no seam, and the existing sphere culling does the rest: the frame goes 11 to 14% down, shadows 14%, scene pass 8%, and the picture is byte-identical.
A second idea, dropping casters under one texel of a cascade, dropped nothing (the smallest caster is a few cm), and its first "win" was run-to-run noise of 30 ms, so I measured twice before
believing the tiles.

## Where the software rasterizer stops being evidence

Sweeping the presets after the terrain tiles: the cascades now draw 730 k triangles instead of 1.73 M and the shadow pass is a floor of about 200 ms for its 4,200 draws at every preset, plus
126 ms of fill at Epic. Both are what a software rasterizer charges and neither is what a GPU does, so the next optimisation (instancing the far cascade's draws) has nothing to be
measured against here and was not built. The roadmap's priority 2 asks for budgets on defined hardware, and this is the point where that hardware is needed: the one measurement to take is
`AETHER_PLAYER_GPU_TIMING=1` on the tiled HeroValley on a real GPU. Recorded in `PERFORMANCE.md`.

## Walking on a baked world

Roadmap rows G02 and G03 are about a character moving over level collision. The engine already had the pieces (a Jolt `CharacterMovement` capsule, a third-person Luau controller, a project template that
wires them on a flat floor), and the baked procedural worlds had none of it: the ground was a model with no collider. `BakeOptions::terrain_collision` now adds a static `MeshCollider` over the ground,
built on the same grid as the drawn ground so the feet meet what is seen. The test drops a capsule on four walkable lines of a baked map and walks it: feet 0.00 to 0.31 m of the sampled ground along
46 m. Two things I got wrong first: the test spots were on 41 to 51 degree hills, where the character correctly slides (a bad test, not bad collision), so spots now need a walkable 10 m line;
and the collider at the drawn grid cost +2.4 s of startup on HeroValley, so it has its own coarser grid (default 192: +0.5 to 0.7 s, mean gap 6 cm). The next step is the character itself (the
entity, its body, the camera, the controller and the input files) in the bake.
One thing I should have noticed sooner: this development tree was configured with `AETHER_BUILD_PHYSICS=OFF`, so every character and physics test was silently not built in my earlier runs and
the ctest gate of 46 checks ran without them. It is on now (CI has it on); the physics-on run found one failure, in a bake test whose entity count the collider changes.

## A baked world you can be in

`BakeOptions::add_player` (on by default where the build has physics, with the collision) puts a person in the baked world: `templates::InstallThirdPersonKit` writes the Third Person template's controller and
bindings into the project (a file already there is kept), the baker adds a Player (a `CharacterMovement` capsule with the script), its body (a 4-colour figure built from tubes, a sphere and a box, 1.8 m, under 700
triangles), a camera, a sun and a sky (a baked scene had none: it was a place to look at, not to be in), on `FindSpawnPoint`'s ground.
The test bakes a world, loads it as the player would (physics, Luau, the bindings) and plays it: the character lands, jumps 1.5 m and comes down, walks 8 m, and the camera is 6.0 m away and 1.4 m above the
ground. It found two real bugs on its first run. The character walked off the edge of the map and fell 36 m and on (the skirt is scenery; nothing collided there): the baker now puts four invisible walls round the map.
And in the steepest preset the spawn was the highest peak: the search had a 15 degree limit and fell back to "highest"; it now relaxes the limit in steps (15, 20, 25, 30, 35 degrees) and falls back to the
gentlest dry sample. A third: the jump check was run after the walk, on a slope the character slides on and cannot jump from; it runs first, where it spawned.
Left: the camera has no obstruction handling (a hill between camera and character); the Input/ folder must be in the project's always-cook list (the panel says so after a bake); the body is a static figure, not animated.

## A camera that stops at the hill, and a figure that walks

The two things the last note listed as left. The Third Person controller (the template's, which the baked world also uses) now sweeps a sphere (0.3 m, `Physics.SphereCast`) from the character's head back toward where the camera
wants to be and stops the camera short of what it hits; it comes in at once and eases back out at 6 per second so it does not pop. `camera_collision` turns it off, `camera_radius` sets the sphere, and it is wrapped in a
`pcall`, so a build without physics keeps the old behaviour instead of failing. Test: with a wall 3 m behind the character the camera sits 2.62 m from its head, 0.3 m (the sphere's radius) in front of the wall's face, and goes back to
6.0 m when the wall is removed; with the option off it goes straight through (the negative control). The test harness now binds the physics scene to scripts, as the player does; before, no template test could have run a physics call.
The figure is now five parts (a body and four limbs, each limb's origin at its joint) and each limb carries `LimbSwing.luau`, which reads how fast the Player is moving from its Transform and swings the limb about X in step: a leg and the arm on its side
half a cycle apart, easing in and out. In the baked world test the limbs are still when it stands, the legs reach 40 degrees in opposition (the arm against the leg on its side) when it walks, and settle within 2 s of stopping.
A still of the packaged world shows the figure standing on the hillside, the pack on its back, the camera behind. It is a stylised figure, not a skinned character: no feet, no hands, no face.

## Killing the editor mid-save, a hundred times

Readiness row A03 said crash recovery was unproven. The pieces were there (the scene writer goes through a temp file, a backup and an atomic rename; `SaveAutosave`, `LoadAutosave`, an in-process test of the recovery) but nothing had ever
died in the middle of a write. `test_scene_crash_recovery.cpp` starts a child process (the test binary again, filtered to a writer test) that loads a scene, adds 1,500 entities of unsaved work and then saves the scene and its autosave in
a loop; the parent waits until it is writing, sleeps a random 0.5 to 30 ms and sends SIGKILL, 100 times, then loads what is left. Over three runs, 9, 11 and 12 kills left a temp file (died mid-write) and in all 300 the saved scene,
its `.bak` and the autosave were whole, loadable files holding the old scene or the new one. A test that cannot fail proves nothing, so a control runs the same loop against a writer that truncates and writes in place: it broke a file on its first kill.
What it does not prove: the Qt editor's restore prompt (a crash report newer than the autosave) lives in `editor/qt/main.cpp`, which this tree does not build, and a real packaged-editor session is untested.
Things I got wrong on the way: I set the test filter with `setenv` in the parent to start the child, and the test framework reads that variable as it goes, so the rest of the suite ran inside the test; the child gets its own environment now. A new scene has five entities of its own, so
the counts are measured, not assumed. And a bigger payload hit the write window less, not more (serialising is the slow part, the write is fast): 5,000 entities gave no mid-write kill in 40, 1,500 gave about one in ten.

## "Not a readable image"

Readiness row A05 asks for "actionable failures for malformed inputs", so I fed the importers a dozen of them and read what came back. The file importer was fair on a missing buffer and bad on the rest, and the loud failures were
the lesser problem: **five malformed models imported as `ok` with no warning** (no `asset` object at all, glTF 1.0, a mesh with no primitives, an accessor reading past its buffer, a file with no meshes), because the loader skipped what it could not read and
said so only in the log, never in `ImportResult::warnings`. The texture importer gave one sentence ("not a readable image") for an empty file, a text file, a truncated PNG and a 64-megapixel one.
Now: the loader keeps a `warnings` list on the scene, every skip and drop names its mesh, primitive and cause (an accessor that reads past its bufferView says how many elements, what stride and how big the view is), `ResolveAccessor` says why where it used to just return false, the JSON parser's own line
and column are in the message (through a SAX handler, because the exception path had crashed once), `asset.version` is required and 2.x only, an index past the vertices is refused, and the importer adds "no meshes" and "not one drawable primitive".
For textures the first bytes say what the file is: a Git LFS pointer (the realistic one: a repo cloned without `git lfs pull` is full of 130-byte "PNGs"), a web page, WebP/TIFF/DDS and friends (converting is the fix), plain text, or a PNG, JPEG or BMP whose own header gives its size, which tells "too big" from "damaged".
`test_import_diagnostics.cpp` has 8 tests; against the old importers 6 fail (the two that pass are "a good file says nothing", which must stay true: all 27 models of the AETHER-01 game and the repo's models and textures import with no warning).
My own bugs on the way: the texture check used `strlen` on a string that starts with a NUL byte (so every unknown file looked like an ICO), and a stream that read a short file reported itself failed (so every file looked unreadable).
Not done: sound has no importer in the registry (`audio/` loads WAVs itself), so A07 is untouched; and texture references from a glTF to a missing image file are not diagnosed here.

## Old files, round trip

Row A04 asks that old files load, "apply migrations, save, reopen, and compare semantic state", and reject what they cannot read without touching the file. The legacy fixtures were only ever loaded, and with the lenient loader, which skips what it does not know: the test would have passed
with components dropped. Reading how the other tools load scenes found the real hole: **the MCP `load_scene` used that lenient loader, and `save_scene` writes the file**, so a scene with a component the server did not know (a plugin's, or one from a newer engine) loaded with a log line, and saving it deleted the component from the
file; an agent would never have been told. The editor already refuses such a scene (`reject_unknown_components`). Now `SceneLoadOptions::unknown_components` collects the names (the loader reads on to name all of them, adds nothing to the world, then refuses), `load_scene` refuses by default naming each one and how to go on,
`allow_unknown_components=true` loads the rest and returns `skipped_components` with a warning that saving over the file deletes them, the server's `--scene` start-up flag does the same, and the editor's message names them instead of "check the Output panel".
Then the round trip: every legacy scene opens in the editor, saves, reopens and saves again. It found one thing at once: `02_Main.ascene` changed on the second save. The editor names the unnamed entities of an old scene ("Entity 2") and treats that form as a placeholder to replace on the next open, but the entity's own placeholder counted as a
name already taken, so on the second open it became "Entity 2 2": every unnamed entity of an old scene was renamed when it was reopened, once. It lets go of its own name first now; a unit test (opens, saves and reopens four times) fails on the old code on all three reopens and passes on the new.
The test compares the original to the resaved file leaf by leaf (a `$v` marker may change, a field the saver now adds is fine, a project is stamped with the current `engine_version`; a key or a value lost or changed fails).
Not done: save game and settings fixtures (nothing from an older release is kept), fixtures older than the v0.27.6 baseline, and fixtures for the older binary formats (scene v1, mesh v1, animation clip v1) that the format table says still load.

## A broken script, 1,000 frames

Row A09 asks to "introduce a known error and show its file, location, and actionable message". I wrote ten broken doors (a syntax error, a nil index, a call of a method that is not there, `error("the door is jammed")`, a failure in `OnCreate`, in `OnStart`, a script that
returns nothing, arithmetic on nil, an endless loop) and read what the script system recorded. Mostly it was already good: `Scripts/Door.luau:4: attempt to index nil with 'boom' (in OnUpdate)`. Three things were not. **Which entity:** with forty doors in a level the line alone does not say which one; the
error now ends `(in OnUpdate, on entity "Front Door" a0b1c2d3)`. **The endless loop** said `script exceeded its instruction budget ... (in OnUpdate)` with no file and no line, because an interrupt has no Lua caller for `luaL_error` to take a location from; it reads the running function itself now (`lua_getinfo` at level 0) and says
`Spinner.luau:4:`, in the form `ScriptError::Parse` (and so the editor and the MCP tools) already understand. **The flood:** a script that fails in `OnUpdate` fails every frame, and every failure was appended, so 1,000 frames made 1,000 identical entries, a list (and a log) that grows for as long as the game runs.
An error that happens again is one entry now, " (repeated N times)", logged at 1, 10, 100, 1,000..., and the list keeps at most 200 distinct errors (the rest are counted in `DroppedErrors()`, and still logged). Two existing tests had encoded the flood (`Errors().size() == 2` after the same error on two frames) and changed to the new contract.
Not done: the Qt script and Blueprint editors (the coverage ledger lists them as not migrated); hot reload in a packaged game; and a script error is shown by the MCP tools and the log, there is no in-game overlay for it.
